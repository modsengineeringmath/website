The birth of chemical engineering: from soda ash works to unit operations

An oil refinery, a sugar mill and a pharmaceutical plant look like different industries. A chemical engineer sees all of them as built from the same steps. That simple idea is what separates chemical engineering from chemistry.

A chemical industry before chemical engineers

The first chemicals needed in bulk were soda ash, for glass, soap and bleaching, and sulphuric acid, used in nearly every process. Leblanc in France found a way to make soda from salt in 1791, and such works spread across Britain in the early nineteenth century. Their chimneys poured out hydrochloric acid gas that killed crops for miles around, which led to one of the world's first pollution laws, the Alkali Act of 1863, and a corps of works inspectors.

Solvay in Belgium developed the cleaner ammonia-soda process in the 1860s, but to pay its way it had to recover almost all of the expensive ammonia. He did it with continuous absorption towers and a plant in which every unit fed the next in a closed loop: chemical-engineering thinking before the term existed.

Davis sees the repeating pattern

George E. Davis worked as an inspector under the Alkali Act and saw every kind of chemical works in Lancashire. He noticed that however different the products, every plant did the same things: moved liquids and solids, heated and cooled, mixed, distilled, evaporated, filtered and dried. He lectured on this at the Manchester Technical School in 1887 and collected it in his Handbook of Chemical Engineering of 1901.

The year after Davis's lectures, MIT opened a chemical engineering programme in 1888, but at first it was a mix of chemistry and mechanical engineering with no way of thinking of its own.

Unit operations change the teaching

In a 1915 report to MIT, Arthur D. Little argued that any chemical process can be broken into a small number of unit operations, such as grinding, mixing, heat transfer, distillation, absorption, filtration and crystallisation. Teach each one thoroughly from first principles and students can design any plant, instead of learning manufacture product by product.

The 1923 textbook by Walker, Lewis and McAdams made this the standard curriculum worldwide, together with mass and energy balances rooted in Lavoisier's conservation of mass. The chemical engineer's most basic equation, in minus out plus generated equals accumulated, looks exactly like Kirchhoff's current law that electrical engineers apply at the nodes of a circuit.

Key people

Nicolas Leblanc

1742–1806 · France

A physician who won a French Academy prize for making soda from salt. His process founded nineteenth-century heavy chemical industry, but released large amounts of hydrochloric acid gas and sulphurous waste. His factory was seized during the Revolution and he died in poverty.

Ernest Solvay

1838–1922 · Belgium

A largely self-taught chemist who solved ammonia recovery in the ammonia-soda process with continuous absorption towers, gradually displacing the Leblanc process. He used his fortune to fund science; the Solvay physics conferences attended by Einstein and others were his.

George E. Davis

1850–1907 · England

An inspector under Britain's pollution-control law who visited many chemical works and saw that all of them used the same basic operations, such as distillation, evaporation and filtration. He taught those operations as a subject in their own right and is called the father of chemical engineering.

Arthur Dehon Little

1863–1935 · United States

A consulting chemist who founded one of the first engineering consultancies in the United States. His 1915 report proposed teaching chemical engineering by unit operations rather than by product, so that a student who learned distillation once could apply it in an oil refinery, a distillery or any chemical plant.

Warren K. Lewis

1882–1975 · United States

An MIT professor who co-wrote the first unit operations textbook and was one of the oil industry's most important consultants. He helped develop fluid catalytic cracking in the 1940s and taught several generations of American chemical engineers.
