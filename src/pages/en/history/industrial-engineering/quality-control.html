History of quality control: from a one-page control chart to Six Sigma

A one-page memo of May 1924 held a small diagram that changed manufacturing worldwide. Shewhart's control chart says: stop fixing what isn't broken, and act when there is a real cause.

Written by Ajarn ModUpdated About 5 min read
Industrial Engineering1. Scientific management2. Operations research3. Quality control4. Lean production

No two parts are alike

In the era of mass production, quality control meant inspecting every item or checking at the end of the line and throwing out the bad ones, which was expensive and did nothing to improve the process. Western Electric, which made equipment for AT&T's telephone system, had a particular problem: equipment buried underground had to be very reliable, because digging it up was costly. Shewhart was asked to find a better way.

He saw that every process varies. Part of the variation is the system's own, and predictable; part comes from special causes, such as a worn tool or a new batch of material. Adjusting the machine every time a value wanders naturally makes the process worse. The control chart, with a centre line and limits three standard deviations above and below, separates the two kinds. His 1924 memo and 1931 book are the origin of statistical process control (SPC).

Deming, Juran and post-war Japan

During the Second World War the United States trained tens of thousands of engineers to use control charts in munitions plants, but after the war, when everything sold, American industry lost interest. Japan, whose goods had a reputation for poor quality, wanted to change that. The Union of Japanese Scientists and Engineers (JUSE) invited Deming to lecture in 1950. He did not teach only engineers; he asked to speak directly to the heads of major companies and told them quality was management's responsibility.

Juran followed in 1954, stressing quality planning and management. Ishikawa brought everyone on the shop floor in through QC circles and seven tools that frontline staff could use. Taguchi developed robust design. Within thirty years Japanese cars and electronics set the world's quality standard, and in 1980 an NBC documentary, If Japan Can... Why Can't We?, made America rediscover Deming at the age of eighty.

Six Sigma and ISO 9000

In 1986 Motorola engineer Bill Smith proposed a target that makes defects almost vanish: specification limits six standard deviations from the mean, which, allowing for a 1.5-sigma drift in the mean, leaves about 3.4 defects per million. Six Sigma became both a statistical method and a management system for improvement projects using the DMAIC steps, and General Electric made it famous in the 1990s.

In 1987 ISO published ISO 9000, which does not say how good a product must be but requires an organisation to have an auditable quality management system. It became a condition of international trade. Many Thai factories gained ISO 9001 certification from the 1990s, alongside the national TIS standards of the Thai Industrial Standards Institute, set up in 1968.

Key people

Walter A. Shewhart

1891–1967 · United States

A physicist at Western Electric and Bell Labs asked to reduce variation in buried telephone equipment. He separated variation into the natural variation of the system and variation from special causes, and the control chart with three-sigma limits tells you when to step in and fix the process.

W. Edwards Deming

1900–1993 · United States

A statistician who learned from Shewhart and told Japanese executives in 1950 that if they followed quality principles their goods would win world markets within five years. He taught that most quality problems come from the system management builds, not from workers. His PDCA cycle and fourteen points are still taught; America took real notice of him only in 1980.

Joseph M. Juran

1904–2008 · Romania / United States

An engineer who worked at Western Electric's Hawthorne Works and popularised the Pareto principle, the vital few and the trivial many, in quality management. He insisted that quality has to be planned at management level, not merely inspected at the end of the line.

Kaoru Ishikawa

1915–1989 · Japan

A University of Tokyo professor who made quality control the job of everyone in the organisation, not only specialists. QC circles, where frontline staff solve problems themselves, and the fishbone cause-and-effect diagram are used in almost every Thai factory.

Genichi Taguchi

1924–2012 · Japan

An engineer with Japan's telephone system who argued that good design must be robust to uncontrollable noise such as temperature and material variation, and defined quality by a loss function that grows whenever a value departs from target, not only when it leaves tolerance. His methods use orthogonal-array experimental designs.
