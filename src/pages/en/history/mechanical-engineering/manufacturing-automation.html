History of manufacturing and automation: from the lathe to Industry 4.0

Watt's steam engine could not have existed without a machine to bore its cylinders round. The history of manufacturing is the story of machines that build other machines, and of the device electrical engineers in Thai factories use every day: the PLC.

Machines that build machines

Watt's biggest problem was not design but finding someone who could make a cylinder round enough that steam would not leak past the piston. John Wilkinson, a cannon maker, had a boring machine that supported the cutter at both ends, so it could bore a long hole straight and round. Watt's engines then became saleable. The lesson: engineering innovation always waits on manufacturing precision.

Around 1800 Maudslay in London built a screw-cutting lathe whose tool slide followed a lead screw rather than a human hand, so every thread came out the same. His pupil Whitworth proposed a standard thread in 1841, and interchangeable parts became the basis of mass production, developed further in US government armouries in the mid-nineteenth century until it was called the American system.

Mass production

In the late nineteenth century Taylor spent over twenty years systematically testing metal cutting to find the best speed, depth and feed. With White he found that heat-treating tool steel almost to its melting point let tools cut several times faster. That work matters as much as the management work for which he is better known.

In 1913 Ford combined interchangeable parts, division of labour and the moving line borrowed from Chicago meatpackers at the Highland Park plant. Model T chassis assembly fell from about twelve hours to under two. Cars became cheap enough for workers to buy, but the monotony drove staff turnover so high that Ford raised wages to five dollars a day in 1914. The rest of that story is in the industrial engineering section.

NC machines and robots

After the Second World War the US Air Force needed wing parts with shapes too complex to mill accurately by hand. John Parsons proposed controlling the milling machine's position with numbers, and MIT's Servomechanisms Laboratory built a prototype that read punched tape in 1952. The G-code of today's CNC machines descends from that work. The first Unimate robot, in 1961, lifted hot die castings that were dangerous for people; dull, dirty and dangerous work is still the main reason to use robots.

The PLC: from relay panels to programs

Car assembly lines in the 1960s were controlled by panels holding hundreds of relays, and every model change meant rewiring the whole panel over several weeks. In 1968 GM's Hydramatic division specified a controller that was programmable, could survive the dust, heat and electrical noise of a factory, and could be understood by electricians who were not programmers. Dick Morley's team answered with the Modicon 084, named after the company's 84th project.

What made the PLC succeed was ladder logic, which looks like a relay drawing, so anyone who could read a motor-control diagram could program it immediately. PLCs are now in every factory in Thailand, and IEC 61131-3 puts ladder alongside four other languages. After that came CAD, from Sketchpad in 1963, 3D printing in the 1980s, and the Industry 4.0 idea of connecting machines, sensors and data.

Key people

Henry Maudslay

1771–1831 · England

Called the father of the machine tool. His lathe with a lead screw made accurate, repeatable threads possible, and he insisted on true flat reference surfaces and fine measurement. Men trained in his shop, such as Whitworth and Nasmyth, became Britain's leading machine-tool builders.

Joseph Marie Jacquard

1752–1834 · France

His loom read punched cards one by one to choose which threads to lift, so complex patterns could be woven without a person memorising them. Storing instructions on cards passed on to Babbage, Hollerith's tabulators and early computers.

Frederick Winslow Taylor

1856–1915 · United States

As a mechanical engineer he and Maunsel White developed high-speed tool steel and found the tool-life equation \(VT^n = C\) still used to plan machining. As a pioneer of industrial engineering he wrote The Principles of Scientific Management.

Joseph Engelberger

1925–2015 · United States

Met George Devol, holder of a patent for a programmable transfer machine, at a party in 1956, and together they founded Unimation. The first Unimate lifted hot die castings at a GM plant in 1961. He is called the father of industrial robotics.

Richard E. Morley

1932–2017 · United States

Set a challenge by GM in 1968: replace relay panels that had to be rewired for every model change with a controller that was programmable, tough enough for the factory floor and understandable by electricians. The result was the Modicon 084 and ladder logic that looks like a relay drawing, the language technicians and engineers in Thai factories still use.
