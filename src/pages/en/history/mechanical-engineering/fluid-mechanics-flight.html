History of fluid mechanics and flight: from Bernoulli's principle to the jet engine

Eighteenth-century mathematicians proved that air offers no resistance to a moving body, which everyone knew was false. The contradiction took a century and a half to resolve, and in the meantime two bicycle makers learned to fly.

Equations that said there was no drag

After Bernoulli and Euler, mathematicians had elegant equations of fluid motion. But when d'Alembert used them in 1752 to compute the force a fluid exerts on a body moving steadily through it, the answer was zero, even though everyone knows walking into a gale is harder than walking on a calm day. Engineers split into two camps: mathematicians studying ideal fluids, and hydraulicians using experimental formulas with no theory behind them.

Navier in 1822 and Stokes in 1845 added viscosity, giving the correct Navier–Stokes equations, but they were so hard to solve that they were almost useless in practice. Whether they always have smooth solutions is still one of the million-dollar Millennium Prize problems.

Timeline

  1. 1643

    Evangelista Torricelli builds the mercury barometer and finds the speed of water leaving an orifice.

  2. 1738 / 1757

    Daniel Bernoulli publishes Hydrodynamica; Euler gives the equations of inviscid fluid motion.

  3. 1752

    D'Alembert finds the paradox that an inviscid fluid exerts no drag on a body, contrary to experience.

  4. 1822 / 1845

    Navier (1822) and Stokes (1845) add viscosity to the equations: the Navier–Stokes equations.

  5. 1883

    Osborne Reynolds injects dye into water flowing in a glass pipe and sees the change from laminar to turbulent flow.

  6. 1891–1896

    Otto Lilienthal makes over two thousand glider flights, dying in a crash in 1896.

  7. 1903

    The Wright brothers make the first powered, controlled aeroplane flights at Kitty Hawk (17 December).

  8. 1902–1906

    Martin Kutta and Nikolai Zhukovsky give the theory of wing lift from circulation.

  9. 1904

    Ludwig Prandtl introduces the boundary layer in an eight-page paper.

  10. 1930 / 1939

    Frank Whittle patents the jet engine (1930); the first jet aircraft, Hans von Ohain's He 178, flies in Germany (1939).

  11. 1944

    Lewis Moody publishes the Moody chart for pipe friction.

  12. 1947

    Chuck Yeager makes the first supersonic flight in the Bell X-1 (14 October).

Reynolds puts dye in a pipe

In Manchester in 1883 Reynolds injected a fine thread of dye into water flowing through a glass pipe. At low speed the thread stayed straight; as the speed rose it suddenly burst and filled the pipe. He found that the transition depended not on speed alone but on the combination \(\rho vD/\mu\), which has no units. The Reynolds number lets engineers test small models and apply the results to full-size machines, as long as the Reynolds numbers match.

The Wrights and Prandtl, less than a year apart

The Wrights learned from Lilienthal, who died when his glider went out of control. They saw that the real problem was not getting airborne but controlling the machine in the air, and devised wing warping. Finding the accepted lift tables wrong, they built their own wind tunnel in 1901. On 17 December 1903 Orville flew for 12 seconds over 37 metres, and Wilbur covered about 260 metres on the fourth flight that day.

Less than a year later Prandtl presented an eight-page paper at the International Congress of Mathematicians in Heidelberg: viscosity matters only in a thin layer next to the surface, and outside it Euler's equations will do. The boundary layer explained drag, flow separation and wing stall. Together with the lift theory of Kutta and Zhukovsky, it made aerodynamics a calculable subject.

Jets and the speed of sound

Piston engines and propellers run out of breath high and fast. Whittle in England and von Ohain in Germany conceived the gas turbine engine without knowing of each other. The first jet aircraft to fly was Germany's He 178, in August 1939, days before the Second World War began. In 1947 Chuck Yeager flew faster than sound in the Bell X-1. The same kind of gas turbine now generates electricity in combined-cycle plants, Thailand's main type of power station, and computational fluid dynamics lets engineers solve the Navier–Stokes equations by computer rather than by hand.

Key people

Osborne Reynolds

1842–1912 · Ireland / England

One of Britain's first professors of engineering, at Owens College, Manchester. His 1883 dye experiment gave the dimensionless number that tells whether flow is laminar or turbulent. The Reynolds number is the first thing an engineer computes in almost any fluid problem.

Wilbur and Orville Wright

1867–1912 · 1871–1948 · United States

Bicycle shop owners who worked like real engineers. Finding that published lift tables were wrong, they built their own wind tunnel and tested over two hundred wing shapes. What made them succeed first was control: wing warping that let the pilot bank and turn, not merely get airborne.

Ludwig Prandtl

1875–1953 · Germany

Resolved d'Alembert's paradox by splitting the flow in two: a thin layer at the surface where viscosity matters, and an outer flow where it can be ignored. That made drag and lift calculable. He is called the father of modern aerodynamics, and his students founded the field around the world.

Theodore von Kármán

1881–1963 · Hungary / United States

A Prandtl student who explained the alternating vortex street behind a cylinder, which is why overhead power lines vibrate in wind, and who helped analyse the Tacoma Narrows collapse. He founded the Jet Propulsion Laboratory at Caltech.

Frank Whittle

1907–1996 · England

An RAF cadet whose thesis argued that aircraft would have to fly high and fast on gas turbines. He held a patent at 22 but could not get funding. His engine ran in 1937, at about the same time as Hans von Ohain's independent work in Germany.
