History of transportation engineering and surveying: from Roman roads to GPS

Distances stay the same, but the world shrinks every time engineers build a new road, railway or bridge. This page covers how travel changed, and the surveying that has to come before every construction job.

A road that drains

Roman roads lasted because they were thick layers of stone with a cambered surface that shed water to both sides, but they were very expensive. After Rome fell, European roads reverted to dirt tracks that turned to mud every wet season. In the late eighteenth century Pierre Trésaguet in France and Telford in Britain revived roads built on stone foundations, and McAdam proposed something cheaper: small broken stone compacted in layers on well-drained natural ground, with the road raised above its surroundings.

The word tarmac comes from tar plus macadam, from the early-twentieth-century practice of pouring tar on macadam roads to keep down dust and water. The asphalt we drive on every day rests on the same principle: get the water out of the road structure.

Timeline

  1. 312 BC

    Rome begins the Via Appia; Roman roads were layered stone, and some are still usable.

  2. 1615

    Willebrord Snellius uses triangulation to measure distances on the ground systematically.

  3. c. 1816–1820

    John McAdam develops the compacted, well-drained stone road surface (macadam).

  4. 1825

    George Stephenson's Stockton and Darlington Railway opens (27 September).

  5. 1830

    The Liverpool–Manchester Railway, the first intercity line worked entirely by steam locomotives.

  6. 1843

    The Thames Tunnel of Marc and Isambard Kingdom Brunel opens after nearly twenty years of digging with a tunnelling shield.

  7. 1869 / 1914

    The Suez Canal (1869) and the Panama Canal (1914) open.

  8. 1883

    The Brooklyn Bridge opens; Emily Warren Roebling ran the work for her ailing husband.

  9. 1893 / 1896

    The Paknam Railway (1893) and the state railway from Bangkok to Ayutthaya (26 March 1896) open.

  10. 1964

    Japan's Shinkansen, the first high-speed railway, opens (1 October).

  11. 1978–1995

    The first GPS satellite launches in 1978 and the system reaches full operation in 1995, transforming surveying.

  12. 1998

    Japan's Akashi Kaikyō Bridge opens with a 1,991 m main span.

Railways changed what distance meant

Iron rails were used in mines before, but the Stockton and Darlington in 1825 and the Liverpool–Manchester in 1830 made the steam locomotive public transport. Within thirty years Britain had thousands of kilometres of track, and national standard time arrived because timetables had to agree at every station.

Railways set new challenges for civil engineers: long iron bridges, tunnels through hills and under rivers, and huge earthworks. The Brunels' Thames Tunnel used a tunnelling shield that Marc Brunel devised after watching shipworms bore through timber, the ancestor of the tunnel boring machines that built Bangkok's underground lines.

Railways in Thailand

Siam's first railway was the Paknam line from Hua Lamphong to Samut Prakan, about 21 km, opened in 1893 by a Danish-run company. The first state line, Bangkok to Ayutthaya, opened on 26 March 1896 and reached Nakhon Ratchasima in 1900. German engineers built the northern and north-eastern lines and British engineers the southern line, so there were once two gauges, until the whole country moved to metre gauge in the 1920s.

Thai engineers are now building double track across the country, urban rail and high-speed lines on the 1,435 mm standard gauge that Stephenson chose almost two hundred years ago, work in which civil, electrical and control engineering have to cooperate closely.

Surveying: measure before you build

Every civil project starts with knowing where the ground is and how high. In 1615 Snellius measured long distances with a network of triangles: one baseline measured by chain, the rest computed from angles with trigonometry. The method mapped the whole of France and powered the Great Trigonometrical Survey of India, which took nearly seventy years. Thailand set up its mapping department in 1885 to survey its borders in the colonial era.

In the second half of the twentieth century electronic distance meters replaced chains and tapes, and GPS changed everything when it reached full operation in 1995. Centimetre-level RTK positioning relies on the phase of satellite radio signals and on least-squares solutions of systems of equations: Gauss's mathematics from the early nineteenth century.

Key people

Thomas Telford

1757–1834 · Scotland

A shepherd's son who started as a stonemason and built hundreds of roads, bridges, canals and harbours. His Menai Suspension Bridge of 1826 was the first great modern suspension bridge. He was the first President of the Institution of Civil Engineers, nicknamed the Colossus of Roads.

George Stephenson

1781–1848 · England

A colliery engine-man who could not read until eighteen, he built locomotives and laid some of the world's first railways. The gauge he chose, 1,435 mm, became the world's standard gauge; Thailand uses metre gauge.

Isambard Kingdom Brunel

1806–1859 · England

Worked with his father on the Thames Tunnel in his early twenties and nearly drowned when it flooded in 1828. He went on to build the Great Western Railway, bridges and the largest steamships of his day, the model of an engineer who always went beyond the standards of his time.

Emily Warren Roebling

1843–1903 · United States

When her husband Washington Roebling, chief engineer of the Brooklyn Bridge, was disabled by decompression sickness from work in the underwater caissons, she studied structural mechanics, strength of materials and cable calculations, and acted as the link with engineers and contractors for nearly a decade until the bridge was finished.

George Everest

1790–1866 · Wales / India

Superintendent of the Great Trigonometrical Survey of India, which measured a meridian arc of over 2,400 km with theodolites weighing half a tonne. The accuracy of the network made it possible to compute the height of the world's highest peak, later named after him, though he himself objected.
