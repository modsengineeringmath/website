History of structural engineering and materials: beams, columns, concrete and finite elements

The Romans built a dome that has stood for nearly two thousand years without knowing Young's modulus. We can calculate almost everything, and bridges still fall. The history of structural engineering is the story of theory catching up with what builders could build.

Built before it could be calculated

Roman concrete mixed lime with volcanic ash from Pozzuoli, which let it set even in seawater. The Pantheon dome uses progressively lighter concrete towards the top, with porous volcanic stone in place of heavy aggregate, and an open oculus at the crown. Roman builders understood weight and thrust purely from experience. Vitruvius wrote ten books on architecture under Augustus with no strength calculation in them at all.

After the fall of Rome the recipe for concrete was lost for more than a thousand years. Gothic cathedrals used masonry and flying buttresses developed by trial and error over generations. Nobody asked seriously how much load a beam could carry until Galileo.

Timeline

  1. c. AD 126

    The Pantheon's dome in Rome, 43 m across, built of unreinforced concrete with volcanic ash. It is still the largest unreinforced concrete dome in the world.

  2. 1638

    Galileo publishes Two New Sciences, the first analysis of a cantilever's strength (off by a factor, but a start).

  3. 1678

    Robert Hooke publishes his law, ut tensio, sic vis (as the extension, so the force).

  4. 1744

    Euler finds the critical load at which a column buckles.

  5. 1756–1759

    John Smeaton builds the Eddystone Lighthouse and experiments to find a hydraulic lime that sets under water.

  6. 1824

    Joseph Aspdin patents Portland cement.

  7. 1826

    Claude-Louis Navier publishes lectures that apply elasticity systematically to structures.

  8. 1867

    Joseph Monier, a French gardener, patents reinforced concrete flower pots.

  9. 1889

    The 300 m Eiffel Tower is built of wrought iron in about two years.

  10. 1930

    Hardy Cross publishes moment distribution, making rigid frames solvable by hand.

  11. 1940

    The Tacoma Narrows Bridge collapses from wind-induced oscillation (7 November).

  12. 1956–1960

    Turner, Clough, Martin and Topp at Boeing apply finite elements to aircraft wings; Clough names the finite element method in 1960.

Theories of beams, columns and elasticity

In Two New Sciences of 1638, written while he was under house arrest, Galileo stated that a cantilever's strength varies with its width times the square of its depth. That is right, but he assumed the wrong stress distribution over the section and was off by a factor. Hooke gave the law of proportional extension in 1678, and the Bernoullis and Euler developed the theory of beam curvature in the eighteenth century. Euler also found the buckling load of a column in 1744, which engineers largely ignored until slender iron structures began to fail in the nineteenth century.

The person who made it all usable for design was Navier. In his 1826 lectures at the École des Ponts et Chaussées he put the neutral axis in the right place and gave a method for elastic stress. Then came Clapeyron's three-moment equation (1857), the reciprocal theorems of Maxwell and Betti, Castigliano's energy theorems (1879) and Mohr's circle (1882): the content of the structural mechanics courses civil students take in their second and third years.

Cement, steel and reinforced concrete

While building the Eddystone Lighthouse in the 1750s, Smeaton found that limestone containing clay gave a lime that set under water. Joseph Aspdin, a bricklayer in Leeds, patented Portland cement in 1824, named after Portland stone, which it resembled in colour. Mass-produced steel arrived with the Bessemer process of 1856, making the tall steel-framed buildings of 1880s Chicago possible.

Concrete is strong in compression and weak in tension; steel is strong in tension but rusts and buckles. Combining them began with outsiders: Lambot built a concrete boat in 1848 and Monier made flower pots in 1867. Hennebique turned it into a building system in 1892, and Eugène Freyssinet developed prestressed concrete in the late 1920s. Concrete is now the most used material on Earth after water.

From hand calculation to finite elements

Multi-storey, multi-bay frames produce systems of equations too large to solve by hand. Hardy Cross's moment distribution of 1930 got round this by repeated corrections that converge, and ruled design offices for decades. Computers brought the matrix stiffness method and finite elements. The name finite element is Clough's, from 1960, after his work with Turner at Boeing, though the idea of dividing a region into small triangles appears in a 1943 paper by Richard Courant. Structural engineers now use software that solves millions of unknowns, but they still need enough basic mechanics to know when the computer is wrong.

Key people

John Smeaton

1724–1792 · England

An instrument maker turned builder of bridges, canals and harbours. He would not trust a rule of thumb until he had tested it, for example with model water wheels to measure efficiency, and by testing limestones until he found that clay in the stone made lime set under water, the road to cement.

Claude-Louis Navier

1785–1836 · France

An engineer of the French Corps des Ponts et Chaussées. His 1826 lectures moved structural design from estimating the load that breaks a member to computing elastic stress and comparing it with an allowable value, the basis of design for nearly two centuries. His name is also on the Navier–Stokes equations of fluid mechanics.

Gustave Eiffel

1832–1923 · France

His firm specialised in iron bridges and designed the internal frame of the Statue of Liberty. Parisian artists protested that the tower was ugly; he saved it from demolition by using it for radio and weather experiments, and late in life built a wind tunnel for aerodynamic research.

François Hennebique

1842–1921 · France

Developed a reinforced concrete system with beams, columns and slabs cast as one piece and stirrups for shear, patented in 1892 and licensed worldwide through agents. It made reinforced concrete the main building material of the twentieth century.

Hardy Cross

1885–1959 · United States

His moment distribution method let engineers solve rigid frames with many unknowns by repeated local corrections at the joints, with no large system of equations. It dominated design offices for about thirty years before computers, and he applied the same idea to flow in water pipe networks.

Ray W. Clough

1920–2016 · United States

A UC Berkeley professor who worked at Boeing on analysing wings as assemblies of small triangles and named the approach the finite element method in 1960. He went on to apply it to dams and earthquake-resistant buildings.
