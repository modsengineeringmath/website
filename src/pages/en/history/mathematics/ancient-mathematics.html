Ancient mathematics: from Babylonian clay tablets to India's zero

Why does an hour have sixty minutes and a circle 360 degrees? The answer is on clay tablets nearly four thousand years old. Ancient mathematics is not just old; it is still hiding in our watches and calculators.

The sixty still on your watch face

The Babylonians counted in base 60, perhaps because 60 divides evenly by 2, 3, 4, 5, 6, 10, 12, 15, 20 and 30, which makes sharing things out easy. The system travelled through Greek astronomy to us, which is why an hour has sixty minutes and a circle 360 degrees. The tablet YBC 7289, a square with its diagonal marked, gives √2 to about six decimal places, good enough for most engineering work today.

Most of the Egyptian mathematics that survives is in the Rhind papyrus: real problems such as sharing bread among workers, the volume of a granary and the area of a field. Egyptians wrote fractions as sums of fractions with numerator one. That looks clumsy to us, but it is a fair way to divide actual goods.

Timeline

  1. c. 1800 BC

    The Babylonian tablet YBC 7289 shows √2 in base 60, and Plimpton 322 lists number triples that fit the Pythagorean theorem.

  2. c. 1550 BC

    The scribe Ahmes copies the Rhind papyrus, a collection of Egyptian problems on fractions and areas.

  3. c. 570–495 BC

    Pythagoras and his school study number and ratio and discover that √2 cannot be written as a fraction.

  4. c. 300 BC

    Euclid assembles the thirteen books of the Elements, built on axioms and proof.

  5. c. 250 BC

    Archimedes bounds π with 96-sided polygons: 3 10/71 < π < 3 1/7.

  6. c. 240 BC

    Eratosthenes measures the Earth's circumference from the difference in the sun's angle at two cities.

  7. c. AD 150

    Ptolemy writes the Almagest with its table of chords, the forerunner of trigonometric tables.

  8. AD 263

    Liu Hui writes his commentary on China's Nine Chapters, including matrix-style elimination and a value of π.

  9. 499

    Aryabhata writes the Aryabhatiya, including a table of sines (jya).

  10. 628

    Brahmagupta gives rules for zero and negative numbers in the Brahmasphutasiddhanta.

  11. c. 1400

    Madhava of the Kerala school in southern India finds infinite series for π, sine and cosine, some two centuries before Europe.

Greece: from calculation to proof

What the Greeks added was not new formulas but a new question: why is this true? The Babylonians knew the right-triangle relation long before Pythagoras, but his school proved it, and on the way discovered something that troubled them deeply: the diagonal of a unit square is not a ratio of whole numbers.

Euclid gathered the knowledge of his time into the thirteen books of the Elements, starting from a handful of definitions and axioms and proving theorem after theorem in a chain. That style became the standard for all mathematics. Archimedes went further. He bounded π by squeezing a circle between 96-sided polygons, and found the area under a parabola by adding an endless sequence of shrinking triangles: the idea of the limit and the integral, two thousand years early.

China and India: the part Western textbooks skip

China's Nine Chapters on the Mathematical Art, compiled under the Han, contains systems of linear equations solved by laying the coefficients out as a table on a counting board and subtracting rows until a triangle remains. A student learning Gaussian elimination is using the method Liu Hui explained in 263. The book also calculates with negative numbers, using red and black counting rods.

India gave the world two things it could not do without. One is place-value notation with zero; Brahmagupta wrote down the rules for zero and negative numbers in 628. The other is trigonometry based on the half-chord. Aryabhata called it jya. Arab translators transliterated it as jiba, but Arabic script omits vowels, so later readers took it as jaib, meaning a bay or fold, and Latin translators rendered that as sinus. Our word sine is a mistranslation almost a thousand years old. Later the Kerala school in southern India found infinite series for sine and π about two centuries before European mathematicians.

Key people

Euclid

c. 300 BC · Alexandria, Egypt

Almost nothing is known of his life, yet the Elements is among the most reprinted books in history after religious texts. Its strength was not new knowledge but the arrangement: everything proved in sequence from a few axioms, the template for systematic thinking.

Archimedes

c. 287–212 BC · Syracuse, Sicily

Mathematician and engineer at once. He found the area under a parabola by a method very close to integration, worked out the principles of the lever and of buoyancy, and designed machines to defend his city against Rome. He was killed by a Roman soldier when the city fell.

Aryabhata

476–550 · India

Wrote the Aryabhatiya at 23, using place-value notation, giving a sine table and arguing that the Earth spins on its axis. The word sine goes back to his Sanskrit jya, garbled through Arabic into the Latin sinus.

Brahmagupta

598–668 · India

The first to set out clear rules for computing with zero and negative numbers, phrased in terms of fortunes and debts. He also gave the area of a cyclic quadrilateral and methods for quadratic equations.

Liu Hui

c. 225–295 · China

His commentary on the Nine Chapters solves systems of linear equations by elimination on a table of numbers, Gaussian elimination some fifteen centuries before Gauss, and estimates π with a 3,072-sided polygon.

Hypatia

c. 355–415 · Alexandria, Egypt

The first woman mathematician whose life is reasonably well documented. She taught philosophy and astronomy and wrote commentaries on Diophantus and Apollonius. She was killed by a mob in the city's political conflicts.
