History of probability, statistics and computing: from the gaming table to the computer

The mathematics of uncertainty began with a gambler who wanted to know how to split the stakes. Three hundred years later it underpins quality control, communication and AI.

Written by Ajarn ModUpdated About 6 min read

A game that was never finished

In 1654 a gambler, the Chevalier de Méré, asked Pascal how to divide the stakes fairly when a game played to a set number of wins is interrupted. Pascal wrote to Fermat, and the two reached the same answer by different routes: count each player's chance of winning had the game been played to the end. That is the idea of expected value engineers use in every risk assessment.

Cardano had written on dice odds about a century earlier, but his book was published almost a decade after the Pascal–Fermat letters and had no influence. Huygens published the first probability textbook in 1657, and Jacob Bernoulli proved the law of large numbers: repeat an experiment often enough and the observed proportion approaches the true probability.

Timeline

  1. 1654

    Pascal and Fermat exchange letters on dividing the stakes of an unfinished game, the start of probability theory.

  2. 1713

    Jacob Bernoulli's Ars Conjectandi is published after his death, with the law of large numbers.

  3. 1733

    De Moivre approximates the binomial distribution with the bell curve.

  4. 1763

    Thomas Bayes's essay is published after his death by Richard Price.

  5. 1805–1809

    Legendre publishes least squares (1805); Gauss connects it with the normal distribution (1809).

  6. 1822–1843

    Babbage designs the Difference Engine and the Analytical Engine; Ada Lovelace's 1843 notes include a program for Bernoulli numbers.

  7. 1854

    George Boole publishes The Laws of Thought, the algebra of logic.

  8. 1908

    William Gosset at the Guinness brewery publishes the t-distribution under the pen name Student.

  9. 1925 / 1935

    Ronald Fisher publishes Statistical Methods for Research Workers and The Design of Experiments.

  10. 1933

    Andrey Kolmogorov bases probability on measure theory with his axioms.

  11. 1936–1937

    Turing defines the Turing machine; Shannon shows that relay circuits are Boolean algebra.

  12. 1945–1949

    ENIAC runs, von Neumann writes the EDVAC report, and the Monte Carlo method is developed at Los Alamos.

  13. 1957

    IBM's FORTRAN lets engineers write formulas directly as programs.

From the stars to a brewery

The bell curve first appears in de Moivre's work of 1733, as an approximation to the binomial distribution for many coin tosses. What made it central was astronomy. Legendre published least squares in 1805 to fit comet orbits to imperfect observations. Gauss said he had used it earlier, and in 1809 showed it is optimal when errors are normally distributed. The dispute was milder than Newton versus Leibniz, but Legendre resented it for the rest of his life.

Much of modern statistics was born in industry. Gosset worked at the Guinness brewery in Dublin and had to judge barley quality from small samples. He developed the t-distribution and had to publish under the pen name Student because the company did not allow staff to publish under their own names. Fisher, at the Rothamsted agricultural station, created the analysis of variance and the design of experiments, the basis of quality control in industrial engineering. Kolmogorov's 1933 axioms put all of it on rigorous mathematics.

Machines that calculate

Babbage, irritated by errors in hand-computed mathematical tables, designed the Difference Engine in 1822 and then the Analytical Engine, with a store, a mill and instructions on punched cards like those of Jacquard's loom. It was never finished, but Ada Lovelace's notes understood its potential more deeply than anyone else of the time.

In the 1930s three ideas came together. Turing defined computation in 1936. Shannon's 1937 master's thesis showed that relay and switch circuits follow Boole's algebra. Konrad Zuse in Germany built the programmable Z3 in 1941. After the war ENIAC, with over seventeen thousand vacuum tubes, ran in 1945, and von Neumann's report on storing the program in the same memory as the data became the architecture of nearly every computer. FORTRAN in 1957 let engineers write formulas directly as programs, and Python does the same job today in the age of AI.

Key people

Blaise Pascal

1623–1662 · France

Built a mechanical calculator at 19 to help his father, a tax official, and with Fermat founded probability from a gambler's question. The pascal unit of pressure and the Pascal programming language are named after him.

Thomas Bayes

c. 1701–1761 · England

A Presbyterian minister who published nothing major in mathematics during his lifetime. Bayes' theorem, for updating beliefs when new data arrive, is now at the heart of spam filters, medical diagnosis and machine learning.

Ada Lovelace

1815–1852 · England

Lord Byron's daughter translated a paper on Babbage's Analytical Engine and added notes three times its length. They include a procedure for Bernoulli numbers often counted as the first computer program, and the observation that such a machine might manipulate symbols other than numbers, such as musical notes.

George Boole

1815–1864 · England / Ireland

Taught himself mathematics because his family could not afford schooling, yet became a professor at Queen's College Cork. His algebra of true and false was treated as philosophy for eighty years, until Shannon showed it was the language of switching circuits. The AND, OR and NOT gates in every chip are Boolean algebra.

Andrey Kolmogorov

1903–1987 · Russia (Soviet Union)

His slim 1933 book made probability rigorous mathematics with three axioms. He also did major work on fluid turbulence, stochastic processes and the complexity theory of information.

Alan Turing

1912–1954 · England

His 1936 paper defined what computation is, through an imaginary machine reading and writing symbols on a tape, and proved that some problems no machine can solve. In the war he played a leading part in breaking Enigma at Bletchley Park, and his 1950 paper asking whether machines can think is still cited in the age of AI.
