History of power systems: the war of the currents and the birth of the grid

The war of the currents is usually told as Edison against Tesla. The real story is more interesting. The people who made alternating current win included Hungarian, German and Russian engineers, and what actually decided it was the transformer and the three-phase motor.

Electricity is a product, so it needs delivery

Several people made a long-lasting incandescent lamp in the late 1870s, Edison in America and Joseph Swan in England among them. But a lamp is useless if no power reaches the house. What Edison did better than anyone was think through the whole system, from power station to meter. Pearl Street in 1882 supplied 110 V direct current to customers within roughly a mile, and that range was its biggest weakness.

Low-voltage DC cannot be sent far because the \(I^2R\) loss in the wires is too high. The answer is to transmit at high voltage and step it down before it enters the house, and in that era that was possible only with alternating current, because it needed a transformer.

Timeline

  1. 1881

    Godalming in England gets the first public electricity supply, a Siemens generator driven by a water wheel.

  2. 1882

    Edison's Pearl Street station in New York begins supplying 110 V direct current (4 September).

  3. 1884

    Siam switches on its first electric lights at the Chakri Maha Prasat Throne Hall (B.E. 2427).

  4. 1884–1885

    Zipernowsky, Bláthy and Déri at Ganz in Budapest build the first practical closed-core transformer.

  5. 1886

    William Stanley demonstrates an AC lighting system with transformers at Great Barrington for Westinghouse.

  6. 1888

    Nikola Tesla is granted his polyphase induction motor patents, which Westinghouse buys. Galileo Ferraris in Italy finds the rotating magnetic field at about the same time.

  7. 1891

    Dolivo-Dobrovolsky's three-phase system carries power about 175 km from Lauffen to the Frankfurt exhibition.

  8. 1893

    The Chicago World's Fair is lit by Westinghouse alternating current, a turning point in the war of the currents.

  9. 1894

    Bangkok converts its trams to electric traction (B.E. 2437).

  10. 1895–1896

    The Niagara Falls hydroelectric plant starts up and begins sending power to Buffalo in 1896.

  11. 1954

    The first commercial HVDC link connects the island of Gotland to mainland Sweden, built by Uno Lamm's team at ASEA.

  12. 1958–1969

    Thailand creates the Metropolitan Electricity Authority (1958), the Provincial Electricity Authority (1960) and EGAT (1969).

Transformer and motor: the real deciders

The first transformers sold for power distribution came from Gaulard and Gibbs in the early 1880s, but they had open cores and were inefficient. Three engineers at Ganz in Budapest made the closed iron core work in 1884–1885, and with it a parallel connection that allowed any number of customers. In America William Stanley improved the Gaulard–Gibbs design enough to demonstrate a working system for Westinghouse in 1886.

AC still lacked a good motor, and factories wanted power more than light. In 1888 Tesla received his patents for the polyphase induction motor, and Galileo Ferraris in Turin had reached the rotating field at about the same time. The system that became the world standard, though, was the three-phase design of Dolivo-Dobrovolsky at AEG. In 1891 it carried power roughly 175 km from Lauffen to Frankfurt with an efficiency that silenced the sceptics.

The war of the currents, without the myths

In the late 1880s Edison's camp tried to frighten the public away from AC, with animal electrocutions and a campaign for an AC electric chair. All of that happened and was every bit as ugly as it sounds, but it did not change the outcome. The economics of long-distance transmission beat everything. The 1893 Chicago World's Fair ran on Westinghouse power, and the Niagara Falls plant that began supplying Buffalo in 1896 was polyphase.

The argument is not really over. In 1954 Sweden opened the first commercial high-voltage DC link to Gotland, and today HVDC is the default for submarine cables and for joining grids that run at different frequencies. Solar panels, batteries and data centres are all DC. Power electronics has given Edison's old question a new answer.

Why Thailand runs at 50 Hz

Early systems used dozens of frequencies, from 25 Hz for large motors to 133 Hz for lighting. As grids grew, each manufacturer pulled towards its own choice. Westinghouse settled on 60 Hz in America; AEG in Germany chose 50 Hz, which suited the metric system. Countries that bought mainly European equipment, Thailand among them, ended up on 50 Hz. Japan is split down the middle to this day: the east runs at 50 Hz from AEG machines, the west at 60 Hz from GE machines.

Key people

Thomas Edison

1847–1931 · United States

Made electric light a business not by inventing the first lamp but by designing the whole system: generators, underground mains, fuses, meters and a price per unit. His Menlo Park laboratory was a model for the industrial research lab.

Nikola Tesla

1856–1943 · Serbian, born in present-day Croatia / United States

Designed the polyphase induction motor, with no brushes or commutator, which let alternating current drive machinery. The SI unit of magnetic flux density is named after him.

George Westinghouse

1846–1914 · United States

Made his fortune with the railway air brake, saw that transformers let AC travel much further than DC, bought Tesla's patents and built the alternating-current system that competed with Edison.

Mikhail Dolivo-Dobrovolsky

1862–1919 · Russia / Germany

An AEG engineer who built the three-phase squirrel-cage induction motor, the three-phase transformer and the Lauffen–Frankfurt transmission. The 50 Hz systems of Europe and Asia descend from this line of work.

Ottó Bláthy

1860–1939 · Hungary

One of the three Ganz engineers behind the closed-core transformer; the word transformer comes from this team. He also designed the induction-type AC energy meter used worldwide for nearly a century.

Choem Saeng-Xuto

1851–1931 · Siam

A noble in the court of King Chulalongkorn who bought a generator in England and installed it so that the first electric lights in the Grand Palace could be switched on in 1884. He was later ennobled as Chao Phraya Surasak Montri.
