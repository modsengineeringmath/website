    What Is a Laurent Series and How Is It Different from a Taylor Series? (Explained with Pictures)

    A Taylor series writes a function as an endless polynomial around a point where the function is well behaved. In engineering, though, we usually care about the points where a function blows up, such as the poles of a transfer function. A Laurent series fixes this with one idea: allow negative powers. This article compares the two series visually and ends with the reason electrical engineers use Laurent series every day, through the z-transform.

    Why negative powers are needed

    Try to expand ezz2\dfrac{e^z}{z^2}z2ez​ about 0 as a Taylor series: you cannot, because f(0)f(0)f(0) is undefined. But divide the series ez=1+z+z22+⋯e^z = 1 + z + \tfrac{z^2}{2} + \cdotsez=1+z+2z2​+⋯ by z2z^2z2 and the expansion appears at once:

    ezz2=1z2+1z⏟principal part+12+z6+z224+⋯⏟Taylor-like part,0<∣z∣<∞\frac{e^z}{z^2} = \underbrace{\frac{1}{z^2} + \frac{1}{z}}_{\text{principal part}} + \underbrace{\frac12 + \frac{z}{6} + \frac{z^2}{24} + \cdots}_{\text{Taylor-like part}},\qquad 0 < |z| < \inftyz2ez​=principal partz21​+z1​​​+Taylor-like part21​+6z​+24z2​+⋯​​,0<∣z∣<∞

    The first two negative-power terms describe everything that happens near 0: the function blows up like 1/z21/z^21/z2 (a pole of order 2), and the 1/z1/z1/z term has coefficient 1, which is the residue. The rest is an ordinary, harmless power series.

    One function, several series: it is all about the annulus

    This is what confuses students most. f(z)=1(z−1)(z−2)f(z) = \dfrac{1}{(z-1)(z-2)}f(z)=(z−1)(z−2)1​ has poles at 1 and 2. The circles through the poles split the plane around 0 into three regions, and each region has its own series.

    There is one trick: split into partial fractions, f=1z−2−1z−1f = \dfrac{1}{z-2} - \dfrac{1}{z-1}f=z−21​−z−11​, and expand each with the geometric series, where a pole inside the region gives negative powers and a pole outside gives positive powers.

    • (a) ∣z∣<1|z| < 1∣z∣<1: f=12+34z+78z2+⋯f = \tfrac12 + \tfrac34 z + \tfrac78 z^2 + \cdotsf=21​+43​z+87​z2+⋯ (no negative powers = Taylor)
    • (b) 1<∣z∣<21 < |z| < 21<∣z∣<2: f=⋯−1z2−1z−12−z4−z28−⋯f = \cdots - \tfrac{1}{z^2} - \tfrac{1}{z} - \tfrac12 - \tfrac{z}{4} - \tfrac{z^2}{8} - \cdotsf=⋯−z21​−z1​−21​−4z​−8z2​−⋯ (both directions)
    • (c) ∣z∣>2|z| > 2∣z∣>2: f=1z2+3z3+7z4+⋯f = \tfrac{1}{z^2} + \tfrac{3}{z^3} + \tfrac{7}{z^4} + \cdotsf=z21​+z33​+z47​+⋯ (negative powers only)

    You can check by substitution: at z=1.5z = 1.5z=1.5 series (b) converges to f(1.5)=−4f(1.5) = -4f(1.5)=−4, but series (a) diverges there, even though f(1.5) is perfectly ordinary. Drag the test point yourself in the Laurent annulus lab (in Thai).

    • Infinitely many negative powers → essential singularity, e.g. e1/z=1+1z+12z2+⋯e^{1/z} = 1 + \tfrac1z + \tfrac{1}{2z^2} + \cdotse1/z=1+z1​+2z21​+⋯

    The residue: the one number left after integration

    When you integrate a Laurent series around a circle inside its annulus, every term (z−c)n(z-c)^n(z−c)n gives zero except n=−1n = -1n=−1, which gives 2πi2\pi i2πi. So ∮f dz=2πi a−1\oint f\,dz = 2\pi i\,a_{-1}∮fdz=2πia−1​; the number a−1a_{-1}a−1​ is called the residue (what remains). It is also where the “cover-up” trick for partial fractions in the inverse Laplace transform (in Thai) comes from — it is really computing the residue at each pole.

    Why electrical engineers need it: the z-transform

    The z-transform of a digital signal, X(z)=∑x[n]z−nX(z) = \sum x[n]z^{-n}X(z)=∑x[n]z−n, is literally a Laurent series about 0. The signal x[n] is the coefficient of z−nz^{-n}z−n, and what signals-and-systems textbooks call the “ROC” is the annulus of convergence. Take X(z)=zz−0.5X(z) = \dfrac{z}{z - 0.5}X(z)=z−0.5z​:

    • ROC ∣z∣>0.5|z| > 0.5∣z∣>0.5 (outside the pole): X=1+0.5z−1+0.25z−2+⋯X = 1 + 0.5z^{-1} + 0.25z^{-2} + \cdotsX=1+0.5z−1+0.25z−2+⋯ ⇒ x[n]=0.5nu[n]x[n] = 0.5^n u[n]x[n]=0.5nu[n], a right-sided signal
    • ROC ∣z∣<0.5|z| < 0.5∣z∣<0.5 (inside the pole): expand in positive powers ⇒ x[n]=−0.5nu[−n−1]x[n] = -0.5^n u[-n-1]x[n]=−0.5nu[−n−1], a left-sided signal

    Same formula, two signals, different annuli. And the system is stable when the annulus contains the unit circle, which is exactly absolute convergence, ∑∣h[n]∣<∞\sum|h[n]| < \infty∑∣h[n]∣<∞. A full three-region example is in the z-transform section (in Thai).

    Summary: which one to use

    • Approximating, linearising, finding limits → Taylor
    • Behaviour near a pole, residues, contour integrals, inverse transforms → Laurent
    • If the region of interest contains no singularity, the two are the same series

    Frequently asked questions

    When should I use a Laurent series instead of a Taylor series?

    When you need to expand a function around a point where it is not analytic (a singularity), or on a region that has a singularity inside it, such as the annulus 1<∣z∣<21 < |z| < 21<∣z∣<2 of 1/((z−1)(z−2))1/((z-1)(z-2))1/((z−1)(z−2)). If the function is analytic on the whole disc, the Laurent series simply becomes the Taylor series.

    What is the principal part?

    It is the group of negative-power terms ∑n≥1a−n(z−c)−n\sum_{n\ge1} a_{-n}(z-c)^{-n}∑n≥1​a−n​(z−c)−n of a Laurent series. It describes how the function blows up near c: no principal part means a removable singularity, finitely many terms mean a pole, infinitely many mean an essential singularity.

    Why does the residue matter?

    The residue is the coefficient a−1a_{-1}a−1​, the only term that survives integration around a closed curve. That gives the residue theorem ∮f dz=2πi∑Res⁡\oint f\,dz = 2\pi i\sum\operatorname{Res}∮fdz=2πi∑Res, used for hard real integrals, the inverse Laplace transform and the inverse z-transform.

    How is the z-transform related to Laurent series?

    The z-transform is defined as X(z)=∑x[n]z−nX(z) = \sum x[n]z^{-n}X(z)=∑x[n]z−n, which is a Laurent series about 0. The signal x[n] is the list of coefficients and the region of convergence (ROC) is the annulus of the series. The same X(z) with a different ROC gives a different signal.
