    How to Find the Radius of Convergence: 3 Methods, Endpoint Checks and the Complex-Plane Reason

    A power series is like a polynomial that never ends; it represents a function only on an interval around its centre. Half the width of that interval is the radius of convergence R. This article teaches three ways to find R, the endpoint checks where exams award the marks, and the explanation textbooks rarely give: why R has exactly the value it has.

    By Ajarn ModUpdated 24 Oct 2026
    In 30 seconds
    • Method 1, ratio: R=lim⁡∣cncn+1∣R = \lim\left|\dfrac{c_n}{c_{n+1}}\right|R=lim​cn+1​cn​​​ · Method 2, root: 1R=lim⁡∣cn∣n\dfrac1R = \lim\sqrt[n]{|c_n|}R1​=limn∣cn​∣​
    • Method 3, complex plane: R = distance from a to the nearest singularity of the function
    • Always substitute the endpoints x=a±Rx = a \pm Rx=a±R and test them separately

    What the radius of convergence is

    A key theorem says a power series ∑cn(x−a)n\sum c_n(x-a)^n∑cn​(x−a)n can behave in only one way: there is a number R (possibly 0 or ∞) such that it converges inside and diverges outside. It never converges in scattered patches, and never only to the right of a. So the job is just to find R and then check the two boundary points.

    Method 1: the ratio test (used most often)

    Apply the ratio test to the absolute value of the whole term and solve “limit < 1”. You get R without having to remember whether cnc_ncn​ goes on top or at the bottom.

    Example 1

    Find the interval of convergence of ∑n=1∞(x−3)nn 2n\displaystyle\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}\frac{(x-3)^n}{n\,2^n}n=1∑∞​n2n(x−3)n​

    1. ∣an+1an∣=∣x−3∣2⋅nn+1→∣x−3∣2<1  ⟺  ∣x−3∣<2\left|\dfrac{a_{n+1}}{a_n}\right| = \dfrac{|x-3|}{2}\cdot\dfrac{n}{n+1} \to \dfrac{|x-3|}{2} < 1 \iff |x - 3| < 2​an​an+1​​​=2∣x−3∣​⋅n+1n​→2∣x−3∣​<1⟺∣x−3∣<2 ⇒ R=2R = 2R=2

    2. Endpoint x = 1: ∑(−1)nn\sum\dfrac{(-1)^n}{n}∑n(−1)n​ converges (Leibniz) · endpoint x = 5: ∑1n\sum\dfrac{1}{n}∑n1​ diverges

    R=2R = 2R=2 · interval of convergence [1,5)[1, 5)[1,5)

    Example 2 · R equals e

    Find the interval of convergence of ∑n! xnnn\sum\dfrac{n!\,x^n}{n^n}∑nnn!xn​

    1. ∣an+1an∣=∣x∣(nn+1)n→∣x∣e\left|\dfrac{a_{n+1}}{a_n}\right| = |x|\left(\dfrac{n}{n+1}\right)^n \to \dfrac{|x|}{e}​an​an+1​​​=∣x∣(n+1n​)n→e∣x∣​ ⇒ R=eR = eR=e

    2. At x=±ex = \pm ex=±e the size of the terms is n! ennn≈2πn→∞\dfrac{n!\,e^n}{n^n} \approx \sqrt{2\pi n} \to \inftynnn!en​≈2πn​→∞ (Stirling), so both endpoints diverge

    (−e,e)(-e, e)(−e,e)

    Method 2: the root test (for nested n-th powers)

    The Cauchy–Hadamard formula 1R=lim sup⁡∣cn∣n\dfrac{1}{R} = \limsup\sqrt[n]{|c_n|}R1​=limsupn∣cn​∣​ always works, and it is convenient when the coefficients are raised to the n-th power or have gaps (zero coefficients in between) where the ratio method breaks down.

    Example 3

    Find the radius of convergence of ∑n=1∞(nn+1)n2xn\displaystyle\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}\left(\frac{n}{n+1}\right)^{n^2}x^nn=1∑∞​(n+1n​)n2xn

    1. ∣cn∣n=(nn+1)n→1e\sqrt[n]{|c_n|} = \left(\dfrac{n}{n+1}\right)^{n} \to \dfrac1en∣cn​∣​=(n+1n​)n→e1​ ⇒ R=eR = eR=e

    2. At x=±ex = \pm ex=±e: (nn+1)n2en→e1/2≠0\left(\dfrac{n}{n+1}\right)^{n^2}e^n \to e^{1/2} \ne 0(n+1n​)n2en→e1/2=0, so both endpoints diverge

    (−e,e)(-e, e)(−e,e) — the ratio method is much messier here

    Method 3: look from the complex plane (understand why)

    Consider 11+x2=1−x2+x4−⋯\dfrac{1}{1+x^2} = 1 - x^2 + x^4 - \cdots1+x21​=1−x2+x4−⋯. On the real line the function is smooth everywhere and never blows up, yet the series converges only for ∣x∣<1|x| < 1∣x∣<1. Why? Because a power series converges on a disc in the complex plane, and the disc grows until it hits the first singularity, which for 11+z2\tfrac{1}{1+z^2}1+z21​ sits at z=±iz = \pm iz=±i, exactly one unit from the centre.

    Example 4 · no coefficients needed

    What is the radius of convergence of the Taylor series of f(x)=1x2−4x+13f(x) = \dfrac{1}{x^2 - 4x + 13}f(x)=x2−4x+131​ about (a) 0 (b) 2?

    1. Poles of f: z2−4z+13=0⇒z=2±3iz^2 - 4z + 13 = 0 \Rightarrow z = 2 \pm 3iz2−4z+13=0⇒z=2±3i

    2. (a) Distance from 0: ∣2±3i∣=13|2 \pm 3i| = \sqrt{13}∣2±3i∣=13​ · (b) distance from 2: ∣±3i∣=3|\pm 3i| = 3∣±3i∣=3

    (a) R=13≈3.61R = \sqrt{13} \approx 3.61R=13​≈3.61 · (b) R=3R = 3R=3

    Endpoint checklist

    1. Substitute x=a+Rx = a + Rx=a+R and x=a−Rx = a - Rx=a−R into the series to get two numerical series.
    2. If the terms do not tend to 0 ⇒ divergence at that endpoint.
    3. If it has the form ∑1np\sum\tfrac{1}{n^p}∑np1​, use the p-series result · if the signs alternate, use Leibniz (and test the absolute values if the question asks how it converges).
    4. Write the interval with the right brackets: ( ) excludes, [ ] includes.

    Keep these standard examples for comparison: ∑xn\sum x^n∑xn gives (−1,1)(-1, 1)(−1,1) · ∑xnn\sum\tfrac{x^n}{n}∑nxn​ gives [−1,1)[-1, 1)[−1,1) · ∑xnn2\sum\tfrac{x^n}{n^2}∑n2xn​ gives [−1,1][-1, 1][−1,1] — the same radius, but all three endpoint behaviours differ.

    Frequently asked questions

    What is the radius of convergence?

    A power series ∑cn(x−a)n\sum c_n(x-a)^n∑cn​(x−a)n converges when ∣x−a∣<R|x - a| < R∣x−a∣<R and diverges when ∣x−a∣>R|x - a| > R∣x−a∣>R. That number R is the radius of convergence. It can be 0 (convergence only at x = a) or ∞ (convergence for every x).

    How is the radius different from the interval of convergence?

    The radius is a single number. The interval of convergence is (a−R,a+R)(a - R, a + R)(a−R,a+R) together with whichever endpoints converge, which must be checked separately at x = a − R and x = a + R, so the interval can be open, closed or half-open.

    Why can't the ratio test decide the endpoints?

    At ∣x−a∣=R|x - a| = R∣x−a∣=R the limit of the ratio is exactly 1 every time, which is the inconclusive case. Substitute the endpoint value and use another test such as the p-series, Leibniz or n-th term test.

    Can I find the radius without computing coefficients?

    Yes, if you know the function the series represents: the radius about a equals the distance from a to the nearest singularity in the complex plane. For 1/(x2−4x+13)1/(x^2 - 4x + 13)1/(x2−4x+13) the poles are 2±3i2 \pm 3i2±3i, so the radius about 0 is 13\sqrt{13}13​.
