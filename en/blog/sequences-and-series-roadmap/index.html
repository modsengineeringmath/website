    What Do You Learn in Sequences and Series? A Roadmap from Sequences to Laurent Series

    Many students meet sequences and series as dozens of separate formulas and never see that they are one story. This article draws the map first: the whole chapter answers a single question that keeps getting deeper, “what do you get when you add infinitely many numbers?” Each stop on the way has its own question, its main tool and the kind of exam question you will see.

    By Ajarn ModUpdated 24 Oct 2026
    In 30 seconds
    • Sequences → series (limit of partial sums) → does it converge? → series with x (power series) → coefficients from derivatives (Taylor) → negative powers allowed (Laurent)

    The big picture in one figure

    Think of filling a bucket one glass at a time. Each glass is a term ana_nan​; the water in the bucket after n glasses is the partial sum SnS_nSn​. The question is whether the level settles at some height (converges) or overflows forever (diverges). If each glass is half the previous one, the level stops at a finite height. If the glasses shrink like 1, ½, ⅓, ¼, …, the bucket overflows eventually, even though the glasses also keep getting smaller. That small difference is the heart of the whole chapter.

    Two rulers that measure every series

    Almost every convergence test compares an unknown series with one whose behaviour is already known, and there are only two families:

    • The geometric series ∑rn\sum r^n∑rn converges ⇔ ∣r∣<1|r| < 1∣r∣<1 — the yardstick of the ratio and root tests (if the terms shrink faster than some geometric series, the series converges).
    • The p-series ∑1/np\sum 1/n^p∑1/np converges ⇔ p>1p > 1p>1 — the yardstick for ratios of polynomials through the limit comparison test; for example 3n+5n3−2\dfrac{3n+5}{n^3-2}n3−23n+5​ behaves like 3n2\dfrac{3}{n^2}n23​, so it converges.

    Know these two well and the remaining tests become a matter of “finding which one my term looks like”. A step-by-step procedure is in the article How to choose a convergence test.

    Why engineers should care about series

    • The formulas you memorised are first terms of series — T=2πL/gT = 2\pi\sqrt{L/g}T=2πL/g​ comes from sin⁡θ≈θ\sin\theta \approx \thetasinθ≈θ; at a 60° amplitude the true period is 7.3 % longer.
    • All of analogue electronics uses small-signal models — a first-degree Taylor polynomial, rd=VT/IDr_d = V_T/I_Drd​=VT​/ID​.
    • A chip computes sin with additions and multiplications — after range reduction a degree-11 polynomial is enough for float32.
    • A digital system is stable when a series converges absolutely — ∑∣h[n]∣<∞\sum|h[n]| < \infty∑∣h[n]∣<∞, and the z-transform is a Laurent series.

    Common misconceptions

    • “The terms go to zero, so the sum is finite.” Wrong: the harmonic series 1+12+13+⋯1 + \tfrac12 + \tfrac13 + \cdots1+21​+31​+⋯ diverges (it needs 12,367 terms to pass 10, but never stops growing).
    • “The order of addition does not matter.” True only for absolutely convergent series; 1−12+13−⋯1 - \tfrac12 + \tfrac13 - \cdots1−21​+31​−⋯ can be rearranged to any sum (try the rearrangement lab).
    • “If a function is smooth on the real line, its Taylor series converges everywhere.” 11+x2\tfrac{1}{1+x^2}1+x21​ has radius 1 because of the complex singularities ±i (read Radius of convergence).
    • “A function has one Laurent series.” It has one per annulus (read Laurent vs Taylor).

    Frequently asked questions

    Is sequences and series hard, and where should I start?

    The parts where you can find the sum (arithmetic, geometric, sigma notation) are not hard. The hard part is deciding whether a series converges, because you must pick the right tool. Start with sequences and their limits, then the geometric series, which is the yardstick for every convergence test.

    How do power series, Taylor series and Laurent series differ?

    A power series is any series of the form ∑cn(x−a)n\sum c_n (x-a)^n∑cn​(x−a)n. A Taylor series is the power series whose coefficients are f(n)(a)/n!f^{(n)}(a)/n!f(n)(a)/n!, so that it represents a function f. A Laurent series also allows negative powers and represents a function around a singularity, on an annulus.

    Why do engineers need sequences and series?

    To approximate functions by polynomials (small angles, small-signal models), to compute sin and exp on microcontrollers, to analyse digital filters and the z-transform, for Fourier series of signals, and to solve differential equations that have no closed-form solution.
