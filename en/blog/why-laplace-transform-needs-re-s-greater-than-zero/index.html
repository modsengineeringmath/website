    Why Does ℒ{1} = 1/s Come With the Condition Re(s) > 0?

    I get asked this almost every semester. Students have L{1}=1/s\mathcal{L}\{1\} = 1/sL{1}=1/s memorised by the end of week one, but ask where the little Re⁡(s)>0\operatorname{Re}(s) > 0Re(s)>0 after it comes from and the room goes quiet for a while. Here is the shortest honest answer I can give. If you want every step, with labs you can drag around, the full lesson is the place to go.

    By Ajarn ModUpdated 25 Nov 2026
    The 30-second version
    • The definition is an integral to infinity. You only get 1/s1/s1/s if e−sTe^{-sT}e−sT dies away as TTT gets large.
    • Euler's formula splits e−ste^{-st}e−st into a shrinking part e−σte^{-\sigma t}e−σt and a rotating part e−jωte^{-j\omega t}e−jωt, whose size is always 1.
    • So σ alone decides: you need σ=Re⁡(s)>0\sigma = \operatorname{Re}(s) > 0σ=Re(s)>0, and ω can be anything.

    Start from the definition

    An integral with infinity as its upper limit really means: integrate up to TTT, then see whether the answer settles down as TTT keeps growing. Do that for a constant ccc (or c u(t)c\,u(t)cu(t), which gives the same thing in the one-sided transform):

    ∫0Tc e−st dt=cs−cs e−sT\int_0^{T} c\,e^{-st}\,dt = \frac{c}{s} - \frac{c}{s}\,e^{-sT}∫0T​ce−stdt=sc​−sc​e−sT

    The first term is the answer we all remember. The second is left over, and it has to disappear. If it doesn't, we have no right to say the result is c/sc/sc/s. So everything comes down to one question: when does e−sTe^{-sT}e−sT go to zero?

    For real sss that is easy. With s>0s > 0s>0 it decays, with s=0s = 0s=0 it stays at 1 (and the area cTcTcT never stops growing), and with s<0s < 0s<0 it grows exponentially. That is why plenty of first-year books write s>0s > 0s>0 and leave it there.

    Once s is complex, Euler does the work

    The catch is that in the real Laplace transform s=σ+jωs = \sigma + j\omegas=σ+jω, and you can't ask whether a complex number is bigger than zero. Is 2+3j>02 + 3j > 02+3j>0? The question doesn't mean anything. So we pull e−ste^{-st}e−st apart instead, using Euler's formula e−jθ=cos⁡θ−jsin⁡θe^{-j\theta} = \cos\theta - j\sin\thetae−jθ=cosθ−jsinθ:

    e−st=e−σt e−jωt,∣e−jωt∣=cos⁡2ωt+sin⁡2ωt=1e^{-st} = e^{-\sigma t}\,e^{-j\omega t},\qquad |e^{-j\omega t}| = \sqrt{\cos^2\omega t + \sin^2\omega t} = 1e−st=e−σte−jωt,∣e−jωt∣=cos2ωt+sin2ωt​=1

    That line is the whole story. The e−jωte^{-j\omega t}e−jωt part just goes round the unit circle; it never shrinks and never grows. The size of e−sTe^{-sT}e−sT is therefore e−σTe^{-\sigma T}e−σT and nothing else, which goes to zero exactly when σ>0\sigma > 0σ>0. That is where Re⁡(s)>0\operatorname{Re}(s) > 0Re(s)>0 comes from, and it is also why the ROC is a right half-plane with a vertical edge: moving ω up or down changes nothing.

    A quick numerical check: at s=2+3js = 2 + 3js=2+3j, L{5}=5/(2+3j)=1013−1513j\mathcal{L}\{5\} = 5/(2+3j) = \tfrac{10}{13} - \tfrac{15}{13}jL{5}=5/(2+3j)=1310​−1315​j. Split it into the integrals of e−2tcos⁡3te^{-2t}\cos 3te−2tcos3t and e−2tsin⁡3te^{-2t}\sin 3te−2tsin3t, integrate each by parts, and you land on exactly the same numbers.

    What about the line Re(s) = 0?

    This is where people slip, because at s=jωs = j\omegas=jω nothing blows up.     Because the condition keeps coming back. When you invert with the Bromwich integral, the line you integrate along has to sit inside the ROC.     Frequently asked questions

    What is the difference between s > 0 and Re(s) > 0?

    If s is real they say the same thing. In the Laplace transform, though, s = σ + jω is complex, and a complex number cannot be compared with zero. The condition that can be written correctly is Re(s) = σ > 0.

    Why doesn't ω affect convergence?

    Because e−st=e−σte−jωte^{-st} = e^{-\sigma t}e^{-j\omega t}e−st=e−σte−jωt and ∣e−jωt∣=1|e^{-j\omega t}| = 1∣e−jωt∣=1 always, so the size of e−ste^{-st}e−st is just e−σte^{-\sigma t}e−σt. ω makes it rotate, never shrink.

    Why is Re(s) = 0 not allowed when nothing actually blows up there?

    At s = jω the integral up to T keeps going round a circle and never settles on a value, so there is no limit. At s = 0 the area is cT, which grows without bound. That is why the condition has to be strictly greater than zero.
