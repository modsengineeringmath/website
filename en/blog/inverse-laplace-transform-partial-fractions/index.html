How to Find the Inverse Laplace Transform: 3 Partial-Fraction Cases with Examples

After solving an equation in the sss domain you get Y(s)Y(s)Y(s) as a ratio of polynomials, and the final step is to convert back to y(t)y(t)y(t). The standard method that works for almost every problem is to split into partial fractions and then use the table. This article divides the work into 3 cases according to the type of pole.

By Ajarn ModUpdated 16 Oct 2026
30-second summary
  • Factor the denominator → identify the pole type → write the partial fractions → find the coefficients → use the table
  • Distinct poles: use cover-up · repeated poles: include every power of (s+p)k(s+p)^k(s+p)k · complex poles: complete the square
  • If the numerator degree ≥ the denominator degree, do long division first

Case 1: distinct poles (non-repeated real roots)

If F(s)=N(s)(s+p1)(s+p2)F(s)=\dfrac{N(s)}{(s+p_1)(s+p_2)}F(s)=(s+p1​)(s+p2​)N(s)​, it can be written as As+p1+Bs+p2\dfrac{A}{s+p_1}+\dfrac{B}{s+p_2}s+p1​A​+s+p2​B​ where A=[(s+p1)F(s)]s=−p1A = \left[(s+p_1)F(s)\right]_{s=-p_1}A=[(s+p1​)F(s)]s=−p1​​. This is the cover-up method: cover the factor you are interested in, then substitute the value of sss into what remains.

Case 2: repeated poles

A pole repeated kkk times needs a term for every power from 1 to kkk. For example (s+1)2(s+1)^2(s+1)2 must be written as Bs+1+C(s+1)2\dfrac{B}{s+1}+\dfrac{C}{(s+1)^2}s+1B​+(s+1)2C​. If you write only the squared term, the coefficients cannot be matched. The coefficient of the highest power can be found by cover-up, and the remaining terms from derivatives.

Case 3: complex poles (complete the square)

If the denominator is a quadratic that does not factor into real roots, do not split it into complex numbers. Complete the square to get (s+a)2+ω2(s+a)^2+\omega^2(s+a)2+ω2, then arrange the numerator as (s+a)(s+a)(s+a) plus a constant. This matches the cosine and sine pairs with the factor e−ate^{-at}e−at.

    f(t)=e−tcos⁡2t+e−tsin⁡2t=e−t(cos⁡2t+sin⁡2t)f(t)=e^{-t}\cos 2t + e^{-t}\sin 2t = e^{-t}(\cos 2t+\sin 2t)f(t)=e−tcos2t+e−tsin2t=e−t(cos2t+sin2t)

When the numerator degree is too high

Partial fractions work only for proper fractions. If the numerator degree ≥ the denominator degree, do long division first. A constant quotient is an impulse δ(t)\delta(t)δ(t), while a quotient that is a polynomial in sss corresponds to derivatives of the impulse, which usually indicates a model that differentiates its input in a physically unrealistic way. Check the problem again.

Frequently asked questions

What if the numerator degree is greater than or equal to the denominator degree?

Do polynomial long division first so that a proper fraction remains (numerator degree less than denominator degree). For example, s2+1s(s+1)=1+−s+1s(s+1)\dfrac{s^2+1}{s(s+1)} = 1 + \dfrac{-s+1}{s(s+1)}s(s+1)s2+1​=1+s(s+1)−s+1​. The constant 1 becomes an impulse δ(t)\delta(t)δ(t) in the time domain.

When does the cover-up method work?

It is the fastest way to find the coefficient of a distinct pole: multiply both sides by (s+p)(s+p)(s+p) and substitute s=−ps=-ps=−p. For a repeated pole it works only for the coefficient of the highest-power term; the remaining terms need differentiation or equating coefficients.

Do complex poles always have to be split into partial fractions containing j?

No. For a quadratic with complex roots, complete the square so the denominator has the form (s+a)2+ω2(s+a)^2+\omega^2(s+a)2+ω2, then use the pairs for e−atcos⁡ωte^{-at}\cos\omega te−atcosωt and e−atsin⁡ωte^{-at}\sin\omega te−atsinωt. The result is real from the start.
