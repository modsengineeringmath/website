# History of the Toyota Production System and lean: from a loom to Industry 4.0

THภาษาไทยENEnglish
// History · History of Industrial Engineering · 4/4


The production system that changed industry worldwide in the second half of the twentieth century did not start in a car plant. It started with a loom that stopped itself when a thread broke.

Industrial Engineering1. Scientific management2. Operations research3. Quality control4. Lean production

## A machine that knows something is wrong

An ordinary loom keeps weaving when a thread breaks, spoiling the whole roll unless someone is watching. Sakichi Toyoda devised a mechanism that stops the loom the instant a thread breaks. The 1924 Type G worked so well that a British firm bought the patent. The idea had two consequences: one person could tend many looms, and problems came to light the moment they happened instead of hiding in scrap at the end of the line.

His son Kiichiro founded Toyota Motor in 1937. Post-war Japan lacked capital, had no market big enough to build one model in Ford-sized volumes, and had no room for stockpiles. The only option was to build many models in small quantities with nothing waiting between steps, which Kiichiro called just-in-time.

## Ohno, kanban and waste

Taiichi Ohno made just-in-time work. In the 1950s he took an idea from American supermarkets, where customers take what they need and the store restocks only what was taken. He reversed it in the plant: each downstream process collects parts from the process upstream and sends back a kanban card ordering replacement of exactly what was used. This pull system cut work-in-process inventory sharply.

Ohno named seven wastes: overproduction, waiting, transport, unnecessary processing, inventory, unnecessary motion and defects. He considered overproduction the worst, because it hides all the others. Shingo's SMED made die changes fast enough that small batches paid. The whole system took almost thirty years, and the outside world hardly noticed until the 1973 oil crisis, from which Toyota recovered visibly faster than its rivals.

## From Toyota to lean

In the 1980s researchers on MIT's International Motor Vehicle Program compared car assembly plants worldwide and found that plants using Toyota-style methods needed far fewer labour hours, less space and less inventory, with better quality. John Krafcik, a researcher on the programme, called the system lean in a 1988 article, and The Machine That Changed the World (1990) spread the word globally. Lean has since moved into hospitals, software development and services.

In Thailand, Toyota Motor Thailand dates from 1962, and the car and parts industry that grew around it made kanban, kaizen, 5S, TPM and poka-yoke everyday words in Thai factories. Industry 4.0 in the 2010s did not replace lean; it added sensors, data and automation, which is where industrial engineers now work more closely than ever with electrical and control engineers.

## Key people

### Sakichi Toyoda
1867–1930 · Japan
A carpenter's son who watched his mother and grandmother weave by hand all day and invented a series of looms. The 1924 Type G stopped the instant a thread broke, so one person could tend many looms with no spoiled cloth. Stopping the machine at an abnormality is jidoka, one of the two pillars of the Toyota Production System. Money from selling the loom patent to a British firm helped fund the car company.

### Kiichiro Toyoda
1894–1952 · Japan
Sakichi's son, a mechanical engineer who studied car plants in America and Europe and set up an automotive department in the loom company, which became Toyota Motor in 1937. Seeing that Japan could not afford Ford-style stockpiles, he called for making only what is needed, when it is needed.

### Taiichi Ohno
1912–1990 · Japan
The plant manager who spent nearly thirty years turning the Toyoda family's ideas into a working system. He named the seven wastes, such as overproduction, waiting and inventory, and taught people to ask why five times to find the real cause. He often said the system was not a technique but a way of thinking.

### Shigeo Shingo
1909–1990 · Japan
An industrial engineering consultant who worked with Toyota. His SMED method cut die changes on large presses from hours to under ten minutes, making small batches economical, and his poka-yoke devices that make wrong assembly impossible are used on Thai car-parts and electronics lines.

### Eliyahu M. Goldratt
1947–2011 · Israel
A physicist who wrote The Goal, a novel about a plant manager with three months to save his factory. It sold millions of copies and teaches the theory of constraints: a system's output is set by a single bottleneck, and improving anything else does not help.

## Frequently asked questions

What is the Toyota Production System (TPS)?
The production system Toyota developed between about 1948 and 1975 under Taiichi Ohno. It rests on two pillars: just-in-time (make only what is needed, when it is needed, in the amount needed) and jidoka (machines and people stop when something is abnormal), with the continuous elimination of waste as its aim.

Where does the word lean come from?
John Krafcik, a researcher on MIT's IMVP, used it in a 1988 article, and the 1990 book The Machine That Changed the World made it widespread, to describe Toyota-style production that uses fewer resources than traditional mass production.

Where did the idea of kanban come from?
Taiichi Ohno took it from the way American supermarkets restocked shelves in the 1950s, replacing only what customers had taken, and turned it into a pull system in the factory using kanban cards.
