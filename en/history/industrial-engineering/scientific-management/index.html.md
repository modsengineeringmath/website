# Scientific management and work study: where industrial engineering began

THภาษาไทยENEnglish
// History · History of Industrial Engineering · 1/4


Taylor stood over labourers loading pig iron with a stopwatch and announced they could carry nearly four times as much. Whether that was science or exploitation is still argued over, and it is where industrial engineering began.

## One best way

Taylor started at Midvale Steel in Philadelphia as a labourer and rose to foreman. He saw workers deliberately working below their capacity, for fear that faster work would bring a cut in piece rates. His answer was measurement: he timed each element of a job with a stopwatch to find the fastest method and make it the standard.

The best-known example is pig-iron handling at Bethlehem Steel in 1899. Taylor claimed that a worker following his method and rest periods went from about 12.5 to 47.5 tons a day. Later historians found that some of the numbers and the story had been embellished, but the principle, break the job into steps, measure and redesign, remains the foundation of industrial engineering.

## The Gilbreths: a film camera and human motion

Frank Gilbreth came from construction and cared less about time than about motion. He saw bricklayers stooping to the ground for every brick, so he designed an adjustable scaffold that kept bricks at waist height, and motions per brick fell by more than half. Later he and his wife Lillian filmed work with a cine camera and small lights on the workers' hands to record the path of each movement, and broke every job into seventeen or eighteen basic elements such as search, grasp, move, position and inspect.

Lillian added what Taylor lacked: fatigue and the worker's mind. She wrote The Psychology of Management in 1914, and after Frank's death in 1924 worked for almost fifty more years, teaching at Purdue and designing kitchens and equipment for disabled people. Their family life with twelve children became the book and film Cheaper by the Dozen.

## Ford, and the question of people

Ford's moving line in 1913 took these ideas as far as they could go: each person repeated one task while the work moved past. Output soared, but in 1913 staff turnover was so high that Ford had to hire several times as many people as he had jobs. The five-dollar day of 1914 was both profit sharing and a way of buying workers' endurance.

The studies at Western Electric's Hawthorne Works between 1924 and 1932 began with a simple question about whether lighting affects output, and found output rose almost every time something changed, whether the light went up or down. The researchers concluded that attention and group relations mattered a great deal. The findings are still debated, but they turned industrial engineering towards human factors and ergonomics, now a major branch of the field.

## Key people

### Frederick Winslow Taylor
1856–1915 · United States
Passed the Harvard entrance but chose an apprenticeship instead, rising from labourer to chief engineer at Midvale Steel. He believed every job has one best way that measurement can find. The idea raised productivity enormously but was attacked for turning workers into machine parts, and in 1912 Congress called him to testify.

### Frank Bunker Gilbreth
1868–1924 · United States
A building contractor who noticed that every bricklayer moved differently. Redesigning the method cut motions per brick from eighteen to about five. He filmed work with a motion-picture camera and broke motion into basic elements called therbligs, his surname spelled backwards.

### Lillian Moller Gilbreth
1878–1972 · United States
A psychologist with a doctorate and mother of twelve. After her husband's death she worked for almost fifty more years, bringing psychology and fatigue into work study, designing motion-saving kitchens, and becoming the first woman elected to the US National Academy of Engineering.

### Henry Gantt
1861–1919 · United States
A colleague of Taylor who felt pure piece rates pushed workers too hard and proposed a bonus scheme rewarding both workers and foremen. His chart of tasks as bars on a time axis planned munitions production in the First World War and is still the standard project management tool.

### Henry Ford
1863–1947 · United States
Brought interchangeable parts, division of labour and the moving line together in one plant, cutting the Model T's price until workers could afford it, and raised pay to five dollars a day in 1914 to stem turnover. His attachment to one model in one colour then lost the market to General Motors in the 1920s.

## Frequently asked questions

What is scientific management?
Frederick Taylor's approach of using measurement and experiment to find the best way to do a job instead of relying on habit, separating planning from execution, selecting and training workers and paying incentives. He set it out in The Principles of Scientific Management (1911).

What is a therblig?
A basic element of motion that Frank and Lillian Gilbreth used to analyse work, such as search, grasp, move, position and inspect. The name is Gilbreth spelled backwards.

Who invented the Gantt chart?
Henry Gantt, an American engineer, around 1910–1915. Karol Adamiecki in Poland used a similar chart from 1896, but it was not published in a widely read language.
