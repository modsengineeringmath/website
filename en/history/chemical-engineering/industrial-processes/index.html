    // History · History of Chemical Engineering · 3/4

    Chemical processes that changed the world: Haber–Bosch, oil refining, plastics and antibiotics

    The food we eat, the clothes we wear, the fuel in our cars and the medicine in the cupboard all pass through processes that chemical engineers scaled up from the laboratory. Some of them have literally changed the size of the world's population.

    Written by Ajarn ModUpdated 12 Nov 2026About 6 min read
    Chemical Engineering1. Origins & unit operations2. Thermodynamics & transport3. Industrial processes4. Process control & safety

    Bread from air

    In the early twentieth century the world relied on Chilean saltpetre for the nitrogen in fertilisers and explosives, and scientists warned it would run out within decades. Air is almost eighty per cent nitrogen, but the N₂ molecule is very stable. Haber used an osmium catalyst at about 200 atmospheres to combine nitrogen and hydrogen into ammonia on a bench apparatus in 1909.

    Making it a plant was harder. Bosch at BASF faced problems nobody had met: steel vessels at high pressure and temperature cracked as hydrogen entered the metal, and osmium was far too scarce. Alwin Mittasch's team tested thousands of catalysts before finding a workable iron one. The first plant, at Oppau, opened in 1913. Haber–Bosch now makes hundreds of millions of tonnes of ammonia a year, uses about one to two per cent of the world's energy, and is why the planet can feed eight billion people.

    Oil and catalytic cracking

    As cars spread, demand for petrol grew faster than plain distillation could supply. Thermal cracking helped, but Houdry found that a clay catalyst gave more petrol of higher octane. The first commercial unit opened in 1937. The catalyst coked up quickly, though, and the unit had to stop periodically to burn it clean.

    Teams from several oil companies, with Warren Lewis and Edwin Gilliland of MIT, solved this by making the catalyst a fine powder that flows like a liquid, circulating continuously between reactor and regenerator. The first FCC unit ran at Baton Rouge in 1942 and made the high-octane aviation fuel that mattered so much in the Second World War. FCC is still the heart of Thailand's refineries today.

    Plastics, nylon and medicine

    The 1930s were the golden age of polymers. Carothers at DuPont made nylon in 1935, and nylon stockings, on sale in 1939–1940, sold out within days. ICI researchers found polyethylene by accident in 1933 when a high-pressure experiment leaked, and in the 1950s Ziegler and Natta found catalysts that make polyethylene and polypropylene at low pressure. Plastics became cheap enough to be in every home, and a waste problem that this generation of chemical engineers has to solve.

    The penicillin Fleming found in 1928 was medically useless until it could be made in quantity. During the Second World War chemical engineers and microbiologists developed deep-tank fermentation, which needed sterile aeration and precise temperature control. Margaret Hutchinson Rousseau designed the first commercial plant. That work began biochemical engineering and the modern pharmaceutical industry.

    Petrochemicals in Thailand

    Thailand imported almost all its oil until the oil crises of the 1970s pushed the government to develop the gas fields in the Gulf of Thailand. PTT was founded in 1978, natural gas first came ashore in 1981, and the gas separation plant at Rayong extracted ethane and propane as petrochemical feedstock. The Map Ta Phut industrial estate grew up under the Eastern Seaboard programme, together with the gas-fired power stations that are now the country's main source of electricity. That growth also raised questions of pollution and community health that remain ongoing work for chemical and environmental engineers.

    Key people

    Fritz Haber

    1868–1934 · Germany

    Won the 1918 Nobel Prize in Chemistry for ammonia synthesis, the source of the nitrogen fertiliser that feeds about half the world's population today. He also led Germany's chemical weapons programme in the First World War. His life is the clearest example that engineering knowledge cuts both ways.

    Carl Bosch

    1874–1940 · Germany

    The chemical engineer who turned Haber's bench apparatus into a plant. The big problem was that hydrogen at high pressure embrittles steel; he solved it with double-walled tubes that let the hydrogen that seeped through escape, while his team tested thousands of catalysts. He shared the 1931 Nobel Prize in Chemistry, the first for industrial high-pressure chemistry.

    Wallace Carothers

    1896–1937 · United States

    A DuPont chemist who proved that polymers are long molecules joined by ordinary chemical bonds, and who made neoprene and nylon. Nylon transformed textiles and became a military material in the Second World War.

    Eugène Houdry

    1892–1962 · France / United States

    A French mechanical engineer with a passion for racing cars who studied catalytic cracking of heavy oil to make better petrol. His process greatly raised the yield of high-octane gasoline, and he later invented a catalytic converter for exhaust.

    Margaret Hutchinson Rousseau

    1910–2000 · United States

    The first woman to earn a doctorate in chemical engineering at MIT and the first woman member of AIChE. She designed the first commercial deep-tank penicillin fermentation plant in 1944, which helped supply enough of the drug for wounded soldiers.

    Frequently asked questions

    What is the Haber–Bosch process and why does it matter?

    It synthesises ammonia from atmospheric nitrogen and hydrogen at high pressure and temperature over an iron catalyst. Fritz Haber achieved it in the lab in 1909 and Carl Bosch scaled it up in 1913. The ammonia is used for nitrogen fertilisers that help feed about half the world's population.

    Who invented nylon?

    Wallace Carothers and his team at DuPont, in 1935. Nylon first went on sale in toothbrushes in 1938 and in stockings in 1939–1940.

    When did Thailand's petrochemical industry begin?

    It took off after natural gas from the Gulf of Thailand came ashore in 1981 and a gas separation plant was built at Rayong, feeding the Map Ta Phut industrial estate under the Eastern Seaboard programme.
