# History of calculus: Newton, Leibniz and the two centuries it took to define a limit

// History · History of Mathematics · 3/6


Calculus was invented twice by two men who never met, then used with great success for nearly two hundred years without anyone being able to define a limit properly. Students struggling with ε–δ may find that reassuring.

## Before Newton: the ideas existed, but not the system

Calculus asks two questions that go back to the Greeks: what is the area under a curve, and how steep is the tangent at a point? Archimedes answered the first for several shapes by adding ever smaller pieces. In the seventeenth century Cavalieri, Fermat and Newton's teacher Isaac Barrow each had special-case methods, but nobody yet saw clearly that the two questions are inverses of each other.

Seeing that relationship, now called the fundamental theorem of calculus, and turning it into a method that works for any function is what Newton and Leibniz achieved.

## Newton and Leibniz: one idea, two languages

Newton worked out his calculus in 1665–1666, when Cambridge closed for the plague and he went home to Woolsthorpe. He thought of variables as quantities flowing in time, called derivatives fluxions and wrote them with dots over the letters. But he kept the work in manuscripts shown only to friends. Leibniz developed his own calculus in Paris in the mid-1670s, thinking in terms of differences between very small quantities, and published first, in 1684.

At first the two corresponded politely. Once their followers began accusing each other of plagiarism, the dispute escalated. In 1712 a Royal Society committee found for Newton, in a report that Newton, as President, had largely written himself. Historians now agree that both arrived at calculus independently, and that Leibniz's notation was better, which is why the world uses it.

## The eighteenth century: it worked before anyone could explain it

The first calculus textbook, of 1696, bears the name of the Marquis de l'Hôpital, but its core came from Johann Bernoulli, whom l'Hôpital paid for lessons and for the right to use his results. L'Hôpital's rule for 0/0 limits is really Bernoulli's. Bernoulli's student Euler then turned calculus into the study of functions and wrote at enormous length on series, differential equations and the calculus of variations.

The foundations, though, were shaky. Was an infinitesimal $dx$ zero or not? In 1734 Bishop Berkeley pointed out that calculus divides by a quantity and then treats it as zero a line later, and called infinitesimals the ghosts of departed quantities. The criticism was fair, and it took mathematicians more than a century to answer it.

## The nineteenth century: a rigorous limit

Cauchy began rewriting calculus in his 1821 textbook for École Polytechnique students, making the limit the central idea instead of the infinitesimal. Bolzano in Prague had similar ideas that nobody read. Riemann gave the definition of the integral in 1854 that engineering textbooks still use, and Weierstrass made the ε–δ definition of the limit precise in his Berlin lectures of the 1860s.

The lesson for engineers is that a tool can work before it is fully explained, but the full explanation is what tells you when the tool will fail. Divergent series, discontinuous functions and limits that cannot be swapped are all traps that nineteenth-century rigour lets us see.

## Key people

### Isaac Newton
1643–1727 · England
Developed calculus at 22–23 for planetary motion but kept it unpublished for decades. Later President of the Royal Society and Master of the Mint, he used those positions to deal ruthlessly with rivals.

### Gottfried Wilhelm Leibniz
1646–1716 · Germany
Philosopher, diplomat and mathematician. His $dy/dx$ and $\int$ notation was so good that everyone adopted it except Britain, which kept Newton's dots for nearly a century and fell behind the Continent. He also built a mechanical calculator and studied binary numbers.

### Leonhard Euler
1707–1783 · Switzerland
One of the most prolific mathematicians in history, even though he was nearly blind for his last seventeen years. He made $f(x)$, $e$, $i$, $\pi$ and $\Sigma$ standard, and turned calculus from the geometry of curves into the study of functions.

### Augustin-Louis Cauchy
1789–1857 · France
A professor at the École Polytechnique who began to rest calculus on the limit rather than on infinitesimals. He also built most of complex analysis; Cauchy's integral theorem and residues are the tools behind the inverse Laplace transform.

### Karl Weierstrass
1815–1897 · Germany
Taught secondary school in small towns for fifteen years before becoming famous, then a professor in Berlin. The ε–δ definition of the limit that first-year students dread comes from his lectures, and he built a function continuous everywhere but differentiable nowhere, which shocked his contemporaries.

### Maria Gaetana Agnesi
1718–1799 · Italy
Wrote the Instituzioni Analitiche of 1748, a clear textbook covering both differential and integral calculus, translated and used across Europe. She was among the first women appointed to a university chair in mathematics (Bologna).

## Frequently asked questions

Who invented calculus, Newton or Leibniz?
Both, independently. Newton developed it first, in 1665–1666, but did not publish; Leibniz developed it in the mid-1670s and published first, in 1684. The dy/dx and ∫ notation we use is Leibniz's.

Is l'Hôpital's rule really l'Hôpital's?
The rule came from Johann Bernoulli, whom l'Hôpital paid for instruction and for the right to his results. It appeared in l'Hôpital's 1696 book, the first calculus textbook.

Who defined the ε–δ limit?
Cauchy began using ε and δ language in the 1820s, but the rigorous definition found in today's textbooks comes from Karl Weierstrass's lectures in Berlin in the 1860s.
