The formulas engineers use every day are between two hundred and four thousand years old. Some came from taxing farmland, some from watching the sky, some from gambling. This section covers the parts of mathematics that engineering students actually study.

## Overview: mathematics grew out of real problems

People tend to picture mathematics as a pure subject dreamed up in an ivory tower. The history says otherwise: most of it came from jobs that needed doing. Egyptians had to re-survey their fields every time the Nile flooded. Babylonians had to work out interest and calendars. Astronomers needed sine tables, navigators needed logarithms, and telegraph engineers needed a faster way to solve differential equations.

The pages in this section follow the order in which engineering students meet the material: ancient number and geometry, then algebra, calculus, the Fourier and Laplace transforms, linear algebra, and finally probability and computing, the main tools of an engineer in the age of AI.

## Why engineers should know where mathematics came from

I have taught engineering mathematics for more than a decade, and one pattern repeats. Students who can recite a formula but do not know what problem it solved forget it within a term. Students who know that Fourier invented his series to describe heat flowing along an iron bar understand straight away why the same series describes harmonics in a power system. History does not make a formula easier, but it makes it make sense.

There is also a lesson in humility. Newton and Leibniz fought over who invented calculus first, yet both used it without a rigorous definition of a limit. It took Cauchy and Weierstrass more than a century to finish the job. What we cover in one semester took humanity several generations.

## Mathematics and Thailand

Thai numerals descend from Indian numerals by way of the Khmer script, which makes them cousins of the Hindu–Arabic digits used worldwide. Astronomical calculation in Siam relied for centuries on the Suriyayat treatise. The moment Thais remember with pride is 18 August 1868, when King Mongkut predicted the total solar eclipse at Wakor in Prachuap Khiri Khan more accurately than the visiting French astronomers. That date is now Thailand's National Science Day.

## Timeline

- c. 1800 BCBabylonian clay tablets carry base-60 tables and a value of √2 correct to about six decimal places.

- c. 300 BCEuclid compiles the Elements, used to teach geometry for over two thousand years.

- 628Brahmagupta writes down rules for calculating with zero and negative numbers.

- c. 820Al-Khwarizmi writes his algebra; the words algebra and algorithm come from his book and his name.

- 1637Descartes publishes La Géométrie, joining algebra and geometry.

- 1665–1687Newton and Leibniz develop the calculus independently.

- 1748Euler publishes the Introductio in analysin infinitorum, with Euler's formula.

- 1822Fourier publishes his theory of heat, the source of Fourier series.

- 1858Cayley publishes his memoir on the theory of matrices.

- 1868King Mongkut of Siam accurately predicts the total solar eclipse observed at Wakor (18 August).

- 1933Kolmogorov sets out the axioms of probability.

- 1936Turing defines the Turing machine and the limits of computation.

## Frequently asked questions

Who is the father of mathematics?
There is no official title. The names most often given are Euclid (the father of geometry), Archimedes, widely regarded as the greatest mathematician of antiquity, and al-Khwarizmi (the father of algebra).

Who invented zero?
Zero as a number that can be added, subtracted and multiplied appears clearly in the work of the Indian mathematician Brahmagupta in 628. Earlier the Babylonians and the Maya had a sign for an empty place, but did not treat it as a number.

Why is Thailand's National Science Day on 18 August?
Because on that day in 1868 a total solar eclipse was seen at Wakor, and King Mongkut had predicted it accurately in advance.

Which mathematicians do electrical engineers rely on most?
Leonhard Euler (Euler's formula and complex numbers), Joseph Fourier (Fourier series and transforms) and Pierre-Simon Laplace (whose name is on the Laplace transform) are the three names electrical engineers meet most often.
