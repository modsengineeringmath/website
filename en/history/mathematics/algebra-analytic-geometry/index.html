    Algebra and analytic geometry: from al-Khwarizmi to Descartes and Galois

    Without algebra we would write formulas as whole paragraphs, as medieval mathematicians did. This page tells how the symbolic language students use without a second thought took more than eight hundred years to settle.

    Written by Ajarn ModUpdated 12 Nov 2026About 5 min read
    Mathematics1. Ancient mathematics2. Algebra & analytic geometry3. Calculus4. Fourier & Laplace5. Linear algebra & vectors6. Probability & computing

    Algebra began in a book without a single symbol

    Al-Khwarizmi's book of around 820 has no x, no plus sign and no equals sign. Everything is in words: a square and ten roots equal thirty-nine units, which is x2+10x=39x^2 + 10x = 39x2+10x=39. What he did was sort equations into six standard forms and give a procedure for each, with a geometric figure to confirm it. That makes it the first algebra textbook taught as a system rather than a collection of problems.

    Al-jabr in the title means restoration: moving a subtracted term to the other side, which school students do every day. The author's name in Latin translation was Algoritmi, which became algorithm. Every time an engineer talks about an AI algorithm, they are saying the name of a scholar from Baghdad.

    Timeline

    1. c. 820

      Al-Khwarizmi in Baghdad writes al-Kitab al-mukhtasar fi hisab al-jabr wal-muqabala.

    2. c. 1070

      Omar Khayyam solves cubic equations geometrically with conic sections.

    3. 1202

      Fibonacci introduces Hindu–Arabic numerals to Europe in the Liber Abaci.

    4. 1545

      Cardano publishes the Ars Magna with solutions of the cubic (from del Ferro and Tartaglia) and the quartic (from Ferrari).

    5. 1572

      Bombelli writes rules for working with square roots of negative numbers, the beginning of complex numbers.

    6. 1591

      Viète uses letters for both unknowns and constants, so general formulas can be written.

    7. 1614

      John Napier publishes logarithms; Henry Briggs follows with base-10 logarithms.

    8. 1637

      Descartes publishes La Géométrie as an appendix to the Discourse on Method; Fermat reaches the same idea independently.

    9. 1799–1824

      Ruffini and Abel prove that the general quintic has no solution in radicals.

    10. 1832

      Évariste Galois writes his letter the night before the duel that killed him at 20, laying the foundation of group theory.

    The cubic, the contests and a number that should not exist

    In sixteenth-century Italy mathematicians earned part of their living from public problem-solving contests, so anyone with a secret method kept it. Tartaglia won a contest in 1535 because he could solve cubics. Cardano got the method from him on a promise of secrecy, then learned that del Ferro had found it earlier and published it in the Ars Magna of 1545, together with his student Ferrari's solution of the quartic. Tartaglia never forgave him.

    The formula had a strange feature. For some cubics with three real roots it forced you through the square root of a negative number before giving a real answer back. Bombelli wrote down rules for these odd quantities in 1572. Complex numbers did not appear because anyone wanted them; they appeared because they could not be avoided. Three hundred years later electrical engineers found they were the natural language of alternating current.

    Logarithms and coordinates: two tools in one century

    Late in the sixteenth century Viète began using letters for both unknowns and constants, and general formulas became possible. A few years later, in 1614, Napier published logarithms, which turned long multiplication into addition from a table. Astronomers such as Kepler adopted them at once, and the slide rule that followed stayed in engineers' shirt pockets until the pocket calculator.

    In 1637 Descartes published La Géométrie as an appendix to a work of philosophy. He showed that curves are equations and equations are curves. Fermat reached the same idea at almost the same time but rarely published. Once geometry could be turned into algebra, the road to calculus opened, because the slope of a tangent became something you could compute.

    Key people

    Muhammad ibn Musa al-Khwarizmi

    c. 780–850 · Baghdad

    A scholar of the House of Wisdom in Baghdad. His book teaches step-by-step solution of linear and quadratic equations. Al-jabr means restoration, moving a subtracted term to the other side, and the Latin form of his name, Algoritmi, became the word algorithm.

    Gerolamo Cardano

    1501–1576 · Italy

    Physician, gambler and mathematician. He obtained the cubic formula from Tartaglia under a promise of secrecy, then published it in the Ars Magna after learning del Ferro had found it first, starting a lasting feud. He also wrote one of the first books on probability, drawn from his dice playing.

    John Napier

    1550–1617 · Scotland

    A Scottish laird who spent twenty years computing logarithm tables by hand so that astronomers could multiply by adding. Laplace said logarithms doubled an astronomer's working life, and the slide rule engineers carried until the 1970s rests on the same idea.

    René Descartes

    1596–1650 · France

    Named unknowns x, y, z and constants a, b, c as we still do, and showed that curves can be written as equations. Rectangular coordinates are called Cartesian after the Latin form of his name.

    Pierre de Fermat

    1601–1665 · France

    A lawyer who did mathematics for pleasure. He arrived at analytic geometry alongside Descartes, had a method for maxima and minima close to differentiation, and founded probability theory with Pascal. His Last Theorem took 358 years to prove.

    Évariste Galois

    1811–1832 · France

    Failed the École Polytechnique entrance twice, was jailed for politics and died in a duel at 20. The group idea he used to explain which equations are solvable by radicals became the language of abstract algebra, cryptography and particle physics.

    Frequently asked questions

    Who is the father of algebra?

    Usually al-Khwarizmi, the Baghdad scholar of around 820 whose book gave algebra its name. Some accounts also credit Diophantus of Alexandria.

    Where does the word algorithm come from?

    From Algoritmi, the Latin form of al-Khwarizmi's name.

    Who invented logarithms?

    John Napier published them in 1614, and Henry Briggs developed base-10 logarithms. Jost Bürgi in Switzerland made similar tables independently but published later.
