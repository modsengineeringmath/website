# History of electromagnetism: from lodestones to Maxwell's equations

Every law in an electromagnetics course has an owner, and most of them appeared in a span of little more than sixty years, between Volta's battery in 1800 and Maxwell's paper of 1865. This page shows how the pieces fit together.

## Electricity and magnetism used to be separate subjects

Before 1820 almost every natural philosopher believed that electricity and magnetism had nothing to do with each other. Static electricity made sparks; a magnet pointed north. William Gilbert, physician to Elizabeth I, drew a firm line between the two in De Magnete (1600), and coined the Latin electricus from which the word electricity comes.

The eighteenth century made electricity measurable. The Leyden jar stored charge for later. Franklin showed that lightning was the same thing as the spark from a jar. Coulomb's torsion balance gave an inverse-square law that looked uncannily like Newton's law of gravitation. All of this, though, was electricity at rest.

## Timeline

- c. 600 BCThales of Miletus is said to have noticed that rubbed amber attracts straw. The word electron comes from the Greek for amber.

- 1600William Gilbert publishes De Magnete, separates magnetism from static electricity and argues that the Earth itself is a great magnet.

- 1745–1746The Leyden jar, the first capacitor, is invented independently by von Kleist and van Musschenbroek.

- 1752Benjamin Franklin shows that lightning is electrical and introduces the positive and negative charge convention still in use.

- 1785Charles-Augustin de Coulomb measures the force between charges with a torsion balance and finds the inverse-square law.

- 1800Alessandro Volta builds the voltaic pile, the first battery to give a steady current.

- 1820Hans Christian Ørsted sees a compass needle deflect near a current-carrying wire. Within weeks Ampère turns the observation into a law of force between currents.

- 1831Michael Faraday discovers electromagnetic induction (29 August) and builds the Faraday disc, the first dynamo. Joseph Henry finds the same effect in America at about the same time.

- 1834Heinrich Lenz states the direction of induced current, the origin of the minus sign in Faraday's law.

- 1865James Clerk Maxwell publishes A Dynamical Theory of the Electromagnetic Field and calculates that electromagnetic waves travel at the speed of light.

- 1884–1885Oliver Heaviside rewrites Maxwell's theory in vector notation as the four equations found in today's textbooks.

- 1887–1888Heinrich Hertz generates and detects radio waves in the laboratory, confirming Maxwell's theory.

## A steady current changed everything

The quarrel between Galvani and Volta over twitching frogs' legs had an outcome neither expected. To show that the electricity did not come from animal tissue, Volta stacked two kinds of metal disc with brine-soaked cloth in between. The pile delivered a continuous current. Experimenters across Europe built copies, and within the same year someone had used one to split water into hydrogen and oxygen.

In April 1820 Ørsted showed his students that a compass needle swings when placed near a wire carrying current. The popular story of an accidental discovery mid-lecture is probably too neat, since he had suspected a link for years, but the result was clear enough for Ampère to start work the moment the news reached Paris. Ampère soon showed that two current-carrying wires attract or repel with no magnet present at all. The SI ampere was defined through exactly that force until 2019.

## Faraday and induction

If a current produces magnetism, magnetism ought to produce a current. Faraday spent the better part of a decade looking. What everyone had missed was that the magnetism has to change. On 29 August 1831 he wound two coils on one iron ring and saw his galvanometer kick only at the moment he closed or opened the first circuit. That ring was the world's first transformer.

Faraday left more than a law. He left a way of thinking. He saw the space around a magnet as filled with lines of force, and he did not believe that forces leap across empty distance, as the mathematicians of his day assumed. Many contemporaries thought the picture unscientific. The person who took it seriously was Maxwell.

## Maxwell pulls it into one theory

Maxwell began with a mechanical model of the lines of force, built from vortices and idler wheels, which looks odd to us now. Along the way he found that Ampère's law was missing a term: the displacement current in a capacitor. With that term added, the equations predicted that electric and magnetic fields would pass energy back and forth as a wave, and the speed came out close to the measured speed of light. He concluded that light itself is an electromagnetic wave.

The 1865 paper ran to twenty equations written out in components and was hard going. The four compact equations in today's books are Heaviside's, rewritten with divergence and curl. Hertz was the one who actually produced radio waves and detected them, in 1887–1888. A student who finds electromagnetics difficult is studying something that three generations of the nineteenth century's best minds built together.

## Key people

### Michael Faraday
1791–1867 · England
A blacksmith's son with little schooling who started as Humphry Davy's assistant. Faraday had almost no mathematics, so he reasoned in pictures; his lines of force became the modern idea of a field.

### James Clerk Maxwell
1831–1879 · Scotland
Turned Faraday's lines of force into mathematics, added the displacement current to Ampère's law and found that light is an electromagnetic wave. His paper on governors is also a root of control theory.

### André-Marie Ampère
1775–1836 · France
Heard of Ørsted's result at the Paris Academy and within weeks had a law for the force between two currents. Maxwell called him the Newton of electricity.

### Alessandro Volta
1745–1827 · Italy
Argued with Galvani over animal electricity. To prove the effect came from two different metals he stacked zinc and copper discs separated by brine-soaked cloth, and built the first battery.

### Oliver Heaviside
1850–1925 · England
A self-taught telegraph engineer who condensed Maxwell's theory into four equations, coined the words impedance and inductance, and invented the operational calculus that became the Laplace transform method for circuits.

### Heinrich Hertz
1857–1894 · Germany
Proved experimentally that electromagnetic waves exist and that they reflect, refract and polarise like light. He thought the discovery had no practical use; within a few years Marconi was sending radio across the Atlantic.

## Frequently asked questions

Who discovered electromagnetic induction?
Michael Faraday, in 1831. Joseph Henry in the United States found the same effect at about the same time but published later; the unit of inductance, the henry, is named after him.

How many Maxwell's equations are there?
Textbooks today give four: Gauss's law for the electric and the magnetic field, Faraday's law and the Ampère–Maxwell law. Maxwell's original 1865 paper had twenty equations in component form; the four-equation version is due to Heaviside.

Who proved that electromagnetic waves exist?
Heinrich Hertz, in experiments carried out in 1887–1888. The unit of frequency, the hertz, is named after him.
