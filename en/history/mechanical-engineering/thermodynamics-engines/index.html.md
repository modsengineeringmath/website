# History of thermodynamics and engines: from mine pumps to power stations and air conditioning

THภาษาไทยENEnglish
// History · History of Mechanical Engineering · 2/4


Thermodynamics is the one science born directly from machines. People built steam engines before they knew what heat was, and science then followed to explain why no engine can ever be 100 per cent efficient.

## Flooded mines

By the late seventeenth century English coal mines were so deep that groundwater flooded them, and pumping with horses was too expensive. Savery patented a steam pump in 1698, but it was prone to bursting and could not lift water far. Newcomen, an ironmonger from Dartmouth, built a better engine in 1712: steam filled a cylinder, a jet of cold water condensed it to make a vacuum, and atmospheric pressure pushed the piston down. It burned a lot of coal, but at a pithead coal was cheap, and hundreds were built.

Watt was given a Newcomen model to repair in 1763–1764 and found that most of the heat went into warming and cooling the cylinder each stroke. The idea of a separate condenser came to him on a walk on Glasgow Green in 1765, but it took another decade, and John Wilkinson's cylinder-boring machine to make cylinders round enough, before Boulton and Watt engines went on sale in 1776.

## Carnot asked the right question

France lost the Napoleonic wars partly because its industry could not match Britain's. Sadi Carnot, son of a famous politician and mathematician, asked how good a heat engine could possibly be. His 1824 answer was that a heat engine is like a water wheel: work comes from heat falling from a high temperature to a low one, so the maximum efficiency depends only on those two temperatures.

Carnot still believed heat was an indestructible fluid, which was wrong, but his main conclusion was right. Joule showed in the 1840s that work turns into heat at a fixed rate, which contradicted Carnot. Clausius in 1850 and William Thomson resolved the conflict by splitting it into a first law, conservation of energy, and a second law about the direction of heat flow. Today $\eta_{max} = 1 - T_c/T_h$ tells power plant engineers why steam must be as hot as the materials will stand.

## Engines and turbines

An internal combustion engine burns its fuel inside the cylinder, so it is much smaller and lighter than a steam engine. Otto built the four-stroke engine in 1876; Karl Benz put one in a tricycle and patented the motor car in 1886. Diesel wanted an engine as close to the Carnot cycle as possible, so he compressed air until it was hot enough to ignite the fuel unaided. Diesels became the prime movers of ships, lorries and standby generators.

For power stations, a reciprocating piston is a poor match for a generator that wants fast, steady rotation. Parsons solved that with the multi-stage steam turbine in 1884, letting steam expand gradually through many rows of blades. Coal, nuclear and combined-cycle plants still use steam turbines on his principle, alongside gas turbines.

## Cooling: thermodynamics in reverse

A refrigerator is a heat engine run backwards, using work to move heat from cold to hot. Jacob Perkins patented a vapour-compression refrigerator in 1834 and Carl von Linde made refrigeration an industry in the 1870s. Willis Carrier built an air-conditioning system that controlled both temperature and humidity in 1902. In a tropical country like Thailand, air conditioning is the largest electrical load in homes and buildings, and the place where mechanical and electrical engineers work together, from inverter compressors to solar PV.

## Key people

### James Watt
1736–1819 · Scotland
Instrument maker to Glasgow University, given a Newcomen model to repair. He saw that it wasted coal by heating and cooling the cylinder every stroke; the separate condenser cut coal use by about three quarters. He invented the horsepower to sell engines, and the watt is named after him.

### Sadi Carnot
1796–1832 · France
A young army engineer who published a single book at 28, asking how much work a heat engine can possibly produce. His answer: it depends only on the temperatures of the hot and cold reservoirs, not on the working substance. The book did not sell, and he died of cholera at 36 before anyone recognised its value.

### James Prescott Joule
1818–1889 · England
A brewer's son who experimented at home, stirring water with a paddle driven by falling weights and measuring how much it warmed. He showed that work and heat convert at a fixed rate. Electrical students know him for Joule heating $I^2R$ and the joule.

### Rudolf Clausius
1822–1888 · Germany
Reconciled Carnot's work with Joule's conservation of energy by separating them into two laws, and coined entropy from the Greek for transformation. His line that the energy of the universe is constant while its entropy tends to a maximum sums up the whole subject.

### Rudolf Diesel
1858–1913 · Germany
A student of Carl von Linde who wanted an engine as close to the Carnot cycle as possible, so he compressed air until it was hot enough to ignite the fuel with no spark plug. The diesel engine is far more efficient than the petrol engine. He vanished from a cross-Channel ferry in 1913.

### Willis Carrier
1876–1950 · United States
A young engineer asked to stop paper in a printing plant from swelling with humidity and spoiling colour registration. His 1902 system controlled both temperature and humidity, and the psychrometric chart he published in 1911 is still used to design air conditioning, the largest electrical load in Thai buildings.

## Frequently asked questions

Who invented the steam engine?
The first practical piston steam engine was Thomas Newcomen's, in 1712. James Watt did not invent the steam engine; he made it far more fuel-efficient with the separate condenser (patented 1769).

Who is the father of thermodynamics?
Usually Sadi Carnot, who published his theory of heat-engine efficiency in 1824. Rudolf Clausius and William Thomson (Lord Kelvin) put the first and second laws into their modern form.

Who invented air conditioning?
Willis Carrier, an American engineer, designed the first system controlling both temperature and humidity in 1902, for a printing plant in Brooklyn.
