# History of civil engineering: the engineers who built the world, and the theory behind them

THภาษาไทยENEnglish

Civil engineering is the only discipline whose two-thousand-year-old works are still in service, such as Roman bridges and aqueducts. Yet the theory that lets us be confident a structure will stand is only two or three centuries old. This section shows how that gap was closed.

## Overview: from master builders to engineers who calculate

People built the pyramids, the Great Wall and the Roman aqueducts without a single structural equation. Builders worked from rules of experience, such as proportions for arches that did not fall, and the ones that did fall are no longer there for us to see. That kind of knowledge works for familiar materials and forms, but not for something nobody has built before.

The term civil engineer appeared in England in the mid-eighteenth century, when John Smeaton used it to distinguish himself from military engineers, the only kind there had been. France opened the world's first engineering school, the École des Ponts et Chaussées, in 1747, and Britain founded the Institution of Civil Engineers in 1818, whose 1828 charter defined engineering as the art of directing the great sources of power in nature for the use and convenience of man.

## Civil engineering learns from failure

The discipline's shared memory is a list of collapses. The Tay Bridge in Scotland fell in a storm in 1879 because wind load had not been properly allowed for. The Quebec Bridge collapsed twice during construction, in 1907 and 1916. The Tacoma Narrows Bridge twisted itself apart in 1940 in a wind well short of a gale. Each produced new theory or new codes. The iron ring Canadian engineers wear on the little finger as a reminder of responsibility is linked by tradition to the Quebec Bridge.

## Civil works in Thailand

Modern civil engineering in Thailand began in the reign of King Chulalongkorn. The first railway was the private Paknam line from Bangkok to Samut Prakan, opened in 1893, and the first state line, Bangkok to Ayutthaya, opened on 26 March 1896, still the State Railway's founding day. For irrigation, the government hired the Dutch engineer J. Homan van der Heide to plan the Chao Phraya basin in 1902, but the Chao Phraya Dam at Chai Nat was completed only in 1957, more than fifty years after his first proposal.

The problem Thai civil engineers know best is Bangkok's soft clay, which subsides badly when groundwater is pumped. Research on Bangkok clay at the Asian Institute of Technology is cited worldwide, and Suvarnabhumi Airport was built on ground that first had to be improved with preloading and vertical drains.

## Frequently asked questions

Who was the first civil engineer?
John Smeaton (1724–1792) of England was the first to call himself a civil engineer, to distinguish his work from military engineering. He built the Eddystone Lighthouse and founded the Society of Civil Engineers in 1771, and is often called the father of civil engineering.

What was the world's first engineering school?
The École des Ponts et Chaussées in France, founded in 1747 to train engineers for bridges and roads.

What was Thailand's first railway?
The private Paknam Railway (Bangkok–Samut Prakan), opened in 1893. The first state railway, Bangkok to Ayutthaya, opened on 26 March 1896.

Why is it called civil engineering?
To separate public works for civilians, such as roads, bridges, canals and harbours, from military engineering, which built fortifications and weapons and was the only kind of engineering before the eighteenth century.
