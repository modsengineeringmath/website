# History of soil mechanics and foundations: making soil calculable

THภาษาไทยENEnglish
// History · History of Civil Engineering · 2/4


Every building stands on soil, yet soil is a material the engineer does not choose, never fully knows and that changes with water. That is why soil mechanics was one of the last branches of civil engineering to become a science.

## A tower that leaned before it was finished

The bell tower of Pisa was begun in 1173 and started to lean once it reached the third storey, because three metres below its shallow foundation lay soft clay. Work stopped for almost a century because of war, which by luck gave the clay time to consolidate and gain strength; otherwise the tower would have fallen. Later builders tried to compensate by making the upper storeys lean back, so the tower is slightly curved like a banana.

The story captures everything about soil: it settles slowly under load over a long time, its behaviour depends on water, and no two spots are the same. Earlier builders had foundation know-how from experience, but no theory of how much or how soon a structure would settle.

## Coulomb, Darcy and Rankine

Coulomb studied earth pressure on fortress walls in Martinique and proposed in 1773 that soil strength has two parts: cohesion and a friction that grows with normal pressure. The equation $\tau = c + \sigma\tan\phi$ is still the first one soil mechanics students learn. A century later Mohr gave the stress circle used to check whether soil will fail.

Darcy was not trying to study soil at all. He needed to design sand filters for Dijon's water supply and measured how fast water flowed through sand under different heads. The flow was proportional to the hydraulic gradient, a law with exactly the form of Ohm's law; an electrical student meeting Darcy's law feels at home immediately. Rankine in Scotland gave another earth pressure theory in 1857, used alongside Coulomb's for retaining walls to this day.

## Terzaghi and effective stress

In the early twentieth century nobody could explain why buildings on clay kept settling for ten years or more. Terzaghi answered in Erdbaumechanik (1925). When load is added to saturated clay, the pore water takes it first, then squeezes out very slowly because clay is nearly impermeable, so the load passes gradually to the soil skeleton and settlement is slow. His equation is the diffusion equation, the same as Fourier's heat equation, and he solved it with Fourier series.

The effective stress principle $\sigma' = \sigma - u$ explains much more: landslides after heavy rain, liquefaction of sand in earthquakes, and cities sinking when groundwater is pumped. The first international conference, at Harvard in 1936, made soil mechanics a recognised discipline, and the book Terzaghi wrote with Peck in 1948 is still read.

## Pisa again, and Bangkok clay

By 1990 the Pisa tower leaned so far that it had to be closed. An international team including John Burland of Imperial College used soil mechanics to fix it in a way that sounds backwards: slowly removing soil from beneath the side that was not leaning, so that side settled down to meet the other. By 2001 the top had moved back about 40 cm, and the tower is expected to be stable for centuries.

Bangkok sits on soft clay over ten metres thick. Heavy groundwater pumping in the past lowered the water pressure in the sand layers, raised the effective stress in the clay and caused tens of centimetres of subsidence in many areas, just as Terzaghi's theory predicts. Research on Bangkok clay at AIT in the 1970s and 1980s became a world reference case, which is why Thai engineers are at home with long piles, vertical drains and groundwater controls.

## Key people

### Charles-Augustin de Coulomb
1736–1806 · France
A military engineer posted for nine years to Martinique to build a fort. There he studied earth pressure on walls and friction; his 1773 paper still underlies foundation engineering. Electrical students know him for Coulomb's law of electric force.

### Henry Darcy
1803–1858 · France
The city engineer of Dijon, who built one of the best pressurised water systems in Europe at the time. His experiments filtering water through sand gave the law of flow through porous media, used in soil mechanics, groundwater and petroleum engineering. He also contributed to the Darcy–Weisbach equation for pipe losses.

### Karl Terzaghi
1883–1963 · Austria / United States
Field work in many countries convinced him that soil behaves like neither a solid nor a fluid. While teaching in Istanbul he built his own test apparatus and found the principle of effective stress: the soil skeleton carries part of the load and the pore water the rest. It explained settlement and landslides, and he is called the father of soil mechanics.

### Arthur Casagrande
1902–1981 · Austria / United States
Terzaghi's assistant at MIT who then built the soil mechanics programme at Harvard. The liquid-limit cup that civil students tap in the lab, and the Unified Soil Classification System, come from his work.

### Ralph Peck
1912–2008 · Canada / United States
Co-author with Terzaghi of a textbook used worldwide and consultant on many major projects. His observational method admits plainly that we never know the ground completely, so we measure how it actually behaves during construction and have contingency plans ready.

## Frequently asked questions

Who is the father of soil mechanics?
Karl Terzaghi, the Austrian engineer who published Erdbaumechanik in 1925, setting out the principle of effective stress and the theory of consolidation.

Why does the Leaning Tower of Pisa lean?
It was built on a shallow foundation over soft clay that compressed unevenly. It began to lean during construction in the twelfth century, and between 1990 and 2001 engineers reduced the tilt by slowly removing soil from under the north side.

Why is Bangkok sinking?
Mainly because groundwater pumping lowered pore water pressures, which increased effective stress in the soft clay and made it consolidate, as Terzaghi's effective stress principle predicts.
