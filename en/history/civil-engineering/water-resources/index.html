    History of water resources, hydraulics and sanitation engineering

    The engineering work that has saved more lives than any other is not a drug or a medical device. It is a clean water pipe laid separately from a sewer.

    Written by Ajarn ModUpdated 12 Nov 2026About 6 min read
    Civil Engineering1. Structures & materials2. Soil mechanics3. Water & hydraulics4. Transport & surveying

    Water has to run downhill at just the right slope

    A Persian qanat is an underground tunnel several kilometres long, dug from the foot of a hill to the plain. The slope has to be just right: too steep and the water scours the tunnel, too shallow and it does not flow. Roman aqueducts are the same; some stretches fall only a few tens of centimetres per kilometre. Roman surveyors achieved this with simple levelling instruments, which is at least as impressive as the arched bridges in the photographs.

    Frontinus recorded nine aqueducts serving Rome and wrote about the problems every water engineer still faces: illegal connections, leaks and measuring how much each customer receives. Roman metering went by the cross-section of the outlet nozzle, which was inaccurate because it ignored velocity.

    Timeline

    1. c. 1000 BC

      Persians dig qanats, gently sloping underground tunnels carrying water from hills to plains; some still work.

    2. 312 BC

      Rome builds its first aqueduct, the Aqua Appia.

    3. c. AD 97

      Frontinus, Rome's water commissioner, writes his treatise on the aqueducts.

    4. 1738

      Daniel Bernoulli publishes Hydrodynamica, relating fluid pressure and velocity.

    5. 1769

      Antoine de Chézy proposes his formula for velocity in open channels.

    6. 1854

      John Snow maps cholera cases in London and traces them to the Broad Street pump.

    7. 1858–1875

      After the Great Stink, Joseph Bazalgette builds London's sewer system.

    8. 1889

      Robert Manning presents the Manning formula for open-channel flow, still used for canals and drains.

    9. 1902

      J. Homan van der Heide plans irrigation for the Chao Phraya basin for the Siamese government.

    10. 1914

      Bangkok's piped water supply begins (B.E. 2457).

    11. 1936

      Hoover Dam is completed in the US; its concrete has to be cooled with embedded chilled-water pipes.

    12. 1957 / 1964

      The Chao Phraya Dam at Chai Nat is finished (1957), then Bhumibol Dam (1964), Thailand's first large multipurpose dam.

    Hydraulics becomes a science

    What Frontinus lacked was the relation between pressure, velocity and flow, which arrived in the seventeenth and eighteenth centuries. Torricelli found the speed of water leaving a hole in a tank, Daniel Bernoulli gave the principle named after him in 1738, and Euler wrote the general equations of fluid motion in 1757. Engineers could now calculate flow in pipes.

    For canals and rivers, Chézy proposed a formula in 1769 while designing a water supply canal for Paris, and the Irish engineer Manning offered an easier one in 1889. Manning's formula is still the first one irrigation and drainage engineers in Thailand use for canals and storm drains. Pipe losses use the Darcy–Weisbach equation together with Moody's chart of 1944.

    Cholera and the sewers

    Nineteenth-century European cities emptied sewage into the rivers they drank from. Cholera returned again and again, and most people blamed foul air. In 1854 John Snow showed with a map of cases in Soho that the disease came from water. In the summer of 1858 the Thames smelled so bad that Parliament rushed through funds for sewers. Bazalgette built over 130 km of intercepting sewers that carried waste downstream of the city, and London had no major cholera epidemic after the system was finished.

    Early in the twentieth century sand filtration and chlorination made piped water safe. Bangkok's waterworks began supplying water in 1914, and sanitary engineering became the root of the environmental engineering taught in Thai universities today.

    Irrigation and dams in Thailand

    Rice has been grown in the Chao Phraya basin for centuries, using canals dug for transport and drainage. Modern irrigation began when the government hired van der Heide in 1902. He proposed a diversion dam at Chai Nat feeding canals across the central plain, but it took more than fifty years, and a World Bank loan, before the Chao Phraya Dam was completed in 1957.

    Bhumibol Dam in Tak province, finished in 1964, is a concrete arch dam that stores water, generates electricity and controls floods. Dams like this are where civil engineering meets electrical engineering, because the turbines, generators and transmission lines have to be designed together with the dam itself.

    c. 40–103 · Rome

    Rome's water commissioner, who wrote a treatise describing every aqueduct, its flow, illegal tapping and maintenance. He was proud enough of them to ask how the idle pyramids could compare. It is one of the oldest surviving engineering management reports.

    Daniel Bernoulli

    1700–1782 · Switzerland

    From history's most famous family of mathematicians. His Hydrodynamica of 1738 linked pressure and velocity in a fluid, the principle behind flow meters, wings and water mains. His father Johann was jealous enough to publish a book on the same subject with an earlier date on it.

    John Snow

    1813–1858 · England

    A physician who did not believe cholera came from foul air. He mapped deaths in Soho and found them clustered around the Broad Street pump. The work founded epidemiology and is the reason environmental engineering keeps clean water apart from sewage.

    Joseph Bazalgette

    1819–1891 · England

    Chief engineer of London's main sewers, built after the summer of 1858 when the Thames stank so badly Parliament could hardly sit. He doubled the pipe sizes his calculations called for because he expected the city to grow, and the system is still in use.

    Homan van der Heide

    In Siam 1902–1909 · Netherlands / Siam

    A Dutch irrigation engineer hired by the Siamese government in 1902. He proposed a diversion dam and canal network for the Chao Phraya basin and helped set up the canal department that became the Royal Irrigation Department. His main plan was never approved for lack of funds; he resigned and left in 1909, and a dam along the same lines, the Chao Phraya Dam, was finished only in 1957.

    Frequently asked questions

    When was Bernoulli's principle discovered?

    Daniel Bernoulli published it in Hydrodynamica in 1738, and Leonhard Euler wrote it as a general equation in the 1750s.

    Who discovered that cholera spreads through water?

    John Snow, an English physician, who mapped cases in London's Soho in 1854 and found them clustered around the Broad Street water pump.

    What was Thailand's first multipurpose dam?

    Bhumibol Dam in Tak province, completed in 1964, which stores water for irrigation, generates electricity and helps control floods.
