    Compensator Design by Root Locus

    Design a lead compensator to move the dominant poles to a required location and a lag compensator to reduce steady-state error, using the angle and magnitude conditions, with the op-amp circuit that realises the compensator.

    Taught by Ajarn ModUpdated 12 Nov 2026About 45 min readฉบับภาษาไทย →

    What you can do after this unit

    • Convert performance specifications into a required dominant pole location
    • Design a lead compensator with the angle and magnitude conditions
    • Design a lag compensator that raises the error constant without moving the dominant poles
    • Choose R and C values for an op-amp circuit that realises the compensator

    The picture to keep in mind: if the road does not go there, build a new one

    Unit 9 ended with a problem: the root locus of the plant does not pass through the pole location we want. Changing the gain only slides the poles along the same path. It does not move the path.

    A compensator is a transfer function added to the loop so that its own poles and zeros reshape the root locus. From Unit 9:

    • A zero pulls the locus to the left: faster and better damped.
    • A pole pushes the locus to the right, but a pole at the origin removes steady-state error.

    Second-order formulas are a starting point, not the final answer

    The %OS and tst_sts​ formulas of Unit 6 are exact only for a second-order system with no zeros. A compensated system has extra poles and zeros, so always check by simulation and iterate. Real design is a loop, not a single calculation.

    Compensators in switching power supplies

    The Type II and Type III networks around the error amplifier of a buck converter are lead-lag compensators with an integrator. A Type III network has two zeros to restore the phase lost in the LC filter, which has a low ζ\zetaζ (Example 6.2). The design principle is the same as in this unit, although in that field it is usually done on the Bode plot, the subject of Units 11 and 12.

    Summary of Unit 10

    • A compensator reshapes the root locus by adding poles and zeros.
    • A lead adds positive angle at sds_dsd​ to pull the locus to the left; design it with the angle condition, then the magnitude condition.
    • A lag raises the low-frequency gain by zl/plz_l/p_lzl​/pl​ while hardly moving the dominant poles.
    • Always check by simulation, because the added poles and zeros make the second-order formulas inexact.
    • One op-amp stage can realise either a lead or a lag.
