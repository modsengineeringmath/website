    Stability and the Routh–Hurwitz Criterion

    The definition of BIBO stability, its link to pole locations in the s-plane, how to build the Routh array, the special cases, and finding the range of gain for stability, with examples of a servo and a generator AVR loop.

    Taught by Ajarn ModUpdated 12 Nov 2026About 40 min readฉบับภาษาไทย →

    What you can do after this unit

    • Decide from the poles whether a system is stable
    • Build the Routh array and count the poles in the right half of the s-plane
    • Find the range of gain for closed-loop stability and the oscillation frequency at the stability limit
    • Handle the special cases of a zero in the first column and a row of zeros

    The picture to keep in mind: a howling microphone

    Hold a microphone near a loudspeaker. Sound from the speaker enters the microphone, is amplified and comes out again, round and round until it howls. Turn the volume down and the howl stops. Same loop, same equipment; the only difference is the gain.

    Every feedback control system carries this risk. Unit 7 showed that high gain gives small error. This unit finds the limit on that gain.

    The condition for stability

    A system is stable if and only if every pole lies in the left half of the sss-plane, that is, has a negative real part.

    The reason comes from Unit 2: a pole at s=σ+jωs = \sigma + j\omegas=σ+jω puts a term eσte^{\sigma t}eσt in the response.

    Pole location Term in the response Status
    Left half (σ<0\sigma < 0σ<0) Decays Stable
    On the imaginary axis, not repeated (σ=0\sigma = 0σ=0) Constant, or oscillation of constant size Marginally stable
    Right half (σ>0\sigma > 0σ>0), or repeated on the imaginary axis Grows without bound Unstable

    The closed-loop poles are the roots of the characteristic equation 1+L(s)=01 + L(s) = 01+L(s)=0. The difficulty is that above second order the roots are hard to find by hand. The Routh–Hurwitz criterion gives the number of roots in the right half without finding the roots.

    A necessary condition

    Let the characteristic equation be

    ansn+an−1sn−1+⋯+a1s+a0=0,an>0a_ns^n + a_{n-1}s^{n-1} + \cdots + a_1s + a_0 = 0, \qquad a_n > 0an​sn+an−1​sn−1+⋯+a1​s+a0​=0,an​>0

    If any coefficient is zero or negative the system is unstable (or at best marginally stable), and no array is needed. If all coefficients are positive, stability is still not settled and the Routh array must be built, except for first and second order, where positive coefficients are enough.

    The pattern: cross-multiply the two rows above (lower left times upper right, minus upper left times lower right) and divide by the leftmost entry of the row just above.

    The Routh–Hurwitz criterion

    The number of roots in the right half of the sss-plane equals the number of sign changes in the first column. The system is stable if and only if the whole first column is positive.

    Summary of Unit 8

    • A system is stable if and only if every closed-loop pole is in the left half of the sss-plane.
    • A zero or negative coefficient means instability, but all-positive coefficients do not guarantee stability from third order upward.
    • The number of sign changes in the first column of the Routh array equals the number of right-half-plane poles.
    • A Routh array with the gain KKK as a variable gives the stable range, the ultimate gain and the oscillation frequency at the limit.

    The Routh criterion only says whether a system is stable. The next unit opens Part 3 with the root locus, which shows how the poles move as the gain changes.
