    Second-Order Response and Performance Specifications

    The standard second-order form, the meaning of natural frequency and damping ratio, the four kinds of response, formulas for overshoot, peak time and settling time, with examples of an RLC circuit, a buck converter LC filter, a servo and generator rotor swings.

    Taught by Ajarn ModUpdated 12 Nov 2026About 45 min readฉบับภาษาไทย →

    What you can do after this unit

    • Write a second-order system in standard form and find its natural frequency and damping ratio
    • Classify the response as overdamped, critically damped, underdamped or undamped from the damping ratio or the poles
    • Calculate percent overshoot, peak time and settling time of an underdamped system
    • Convert performance specifications into a region for the poles in the s-plane

    The picture to keep in mind: energy passed back and forth

    A first-order system has one energy store, so energy flows one way until it is gone. A second-order system has two energy stores that can exchange energy. In an LC circuit energy alternates between the electric field of the capacitor and the magnetic field of the inductor. In a mechanical system it alternates between the potential energy of the spring and the kinetic energy of the mass. That exchange is oscillation, and resistance or friction removes energy so the oscillation dies away.

    So this unit asks two questions: how fast does it oscillate, and how fast does the oscillation stop?

    Example 6.1 The RLC circuit of Unit 3

    The RLC circuit of Example 3.1 has ωn=1000\omega_n = 1000ωn​=1000 rad/s and ζ=0.5\zeta = 0.5ζ=0.5. A 5 V step is applied. Find the peak capacitor voltage, when it occurs and the settling time.

    Example 6.3 Choosing the gain of a position servo

    A servomotor has G(s)=Ks(s+4)G(s) = \dfrac{K}{s(s + 4)}G(s)=s(s+4)K​ with unity feedback. Find KKK for 5 % overshoot.

    Step 1. Closed loop: T(s)=Ks2+4s+KT(s) = \dfrac{K}{s^2 + 4s + K}T(s)=s2+4s+KK​, so ωn2=K\omega_n^2 = Kωn2​=K and 2ζωn=42\zeta\omega_n = 42ζωn​=4.

    Step 2. ζ\zetaζ from the overshoot.

    ζ=−ln⁡(0.05)π2+ln⁡2(0.05)=2.9969.870+8.974=0.690\zeta = \frac{-\ln(0.05)}{\sqrt{\pi^2 + \ln^2(0.05)}} = \frac{2.996}{\sqrt{9.870 + 8.974}} = 0.690ζ=π2+ln2(0.05)​−ln(0.05)​=9.870+8.974​2.996​=0.690

    Step 3. ωn=42(0.690)=2.90\omega_n = \dfrac{4}{2(0.690)} = 2.90ωn​=2(0.690)4​=2.90 rad/s and K=ωn2=8.40K = \omega_n^2 = 8.40K=ωn2​=8.40

    The settling time is 4/(ζωn)=4/2=24/(\zeta\omega_n) = 4/2 = 24/(ζωn​)=4/2=2 s whatever KKK is, because the real part of the poles is fixed at −2-2−2. To go faster, changing the gain is not enough; a compensator must be added.

    Rotor swings of a generator

    The swing equation of a synchronous generator connected to an infinite bus, linearised about an operating point, is a second-order system.

    2Hωsd2Δδdt2+DdΔδdt+Ps Δδ=ΔPm,ωn=ωsPs2H\frac{2H}{\omega_s}\frac{d^2\Delta\delta}{dt^2} + D\frac{d\Delta\delta}{dt} + P_s\,\Delta\delta = \Delta P_m, \qquad \omega_n = \sqrt{\frac{\omega_s P_s}{2H}}ωs​2H​dt2d2Δδ​+DdtdΔδ​+Ps​Δδ=ΔPm​,ωn​=2Hωs​Ps​​​

    For H=5H = 5H=5 s and Ps=1.5P_s = 1.5Ps​=1.5 pu on a 50 Hz system (ωs=314\omega_s = 314ωs​=314 rad/s), ωn=47.1=6.86\omega_n = \sqrt{47.1} = 6.86ωn​=47.1​=6.86 rad/s, about 1.1 Hz, which is in the range of the electromechanical oscillations seen on real systems. The natural damping DDD is small, so a power system stabiliser is needed to add damping.
