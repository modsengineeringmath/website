    Digital Control: Microcontrollers, Sampling and Digital PID

    The structure of a digital control loop, ADC, zero-order hold and PWM, choosing the sampling period, aliasing, a first look at the z-transform, stability inside the unit circle, and writing PID as a difference equation for microcontrollers and PLCs.

    Taught by Ajarn ModUpdated 12 Nov 2026About 50 min readฉบับภาษาไทย →

    What you can do after this unit

    • Explain the role of the ADC, the processor and the PWM or DAC in a digital control loop
    • Choose a sampling period and an anti-aliasing filter to suit the loop bandwidth
    • Convert a first-order system to a discrete-time model and test stability with the unit circle
    • Write a PI or PID controller as a difference equation ready to program

    The picture to keep in mind: control in beats

    The op-amp controllers of Units 10 and 13 work continuously. A microcontroller cannot. It works in a cycle: measure, compute, command, then wait, and repeat. Between cycles the controller does not know what the plant is doing, and the command stays at its last value.

    If the cycle is very short compared with the speed of the plant, the result hardly differs from a continuous controller. If the cycle is too long, the loop rings or goes unstable. This unit answers how short is "short enough", and how to write the controller as a program.

    Parts of a digital loop

    Part Function What to watch
    Anti-aliasing filter Analog low-pass filter in front of the ADC Must remove frequencies above half the sampling frequency
    ADC Converts voltage to a number every period TTT Resolution (number of bits) and conversion time
    Processor Computes the difference equation of the controller Computation time is time delay (Unit 12)
    DAC or PWM Converts the number back to a command and holds it for the whole period Acts as a zero-order hold (ZOH)
    Timer interrupt Keeps the period TTT constant A varying period (jitter) makes the I and D gains wrong

    Example 15.1 ADC resolution

    A 12-bit ADC with a 0–3.3 V input range reads a current sensor with a gain of 66 mV/A. Find the size of 1 LSB in volts and in amperes.

    1 LSB=3.3212=3.34096=0.806 mV⇒0.80666=12.2 mA1\ \text{LSB} = \frac{3.3}{2^{12}} = \frac{3.3}{4096} = 0.806\ \text{mV} \quad\Rightarrow\quad \frac{0.806}{66} = 12.2\ \text{mA}1 LSB=2123.3​=40963.3​=0.806 mV⇒660.806​=12.2 mA

    The controller cannot see a current change smaller than 12 mA. With a high controller gain the output jumps in steps as 1 LSB changes, giving a small oscillation that never dies away (a limit cycle). Using the full range of the ADC with signal conditioning therefore matters.

    Choosing the sampling period

    The sampling theorem sets the minimum: the sampling frequency fs=1/Tf_s = 1/Tfs​=1/T must be more than twice the highest frequency in the signal. Otherwise high-frequency components appear as false low frequencies, called aliasing, and cannot be filtered out afterwards.

    For control that minimum is not enough, because the zero-order hold delays the command by T/2T/2T/2 on average, which eats phase margin.

    Δϕ≈ωgc T2×180°π\Delta\phi \approx \omega_{gc}\,\frac{T}{2} \times \frac{180°}{\pi}Δϕ≈ωgc​2T​×π180°​

    Rule of practice

    Choose a sampling frequency of about 10–30 times the closed-loop bandwidth. At 20 times, the phase lost to the ZOH is 2π20×12×180°π=9°\dfrac{2\pi}{20} \times \dfrac12 \times \dfrac{180°}{\pi} = 9°202π​×21​×π180°​=9°, which is acceptable.

    Pressure, flow 0.1–1 Hz 10–100 ms PLC, DCS
    Motor speed 10–100 Hz 0.25–1 ms Drive, microcontroller
    Motor current, converter voltage 0.5–5 kHz 20–100 µs (often the PWM period) Microcontroller, DSP

    Example 15.3 The motor speed PI in a microcontroller

    Program the PI controller of Example 13.1 (Kp=0.20K_p = 0.20Kp​=0.20, Ki=2.70K_i = 2.70Ki​=2.70) with a period T=1T = 1T=1 ms. Find the coefficients of the velocity form and check that the period is suitable.

    Step 1. KiT=2.70×0.001=0.0027K_iT = 2.70 \times 0.001 = 0.0027Ki​T=2.70×0.001=0.0027

    u[k]=u[k−1]+0.20(e[k]−e[k−1])+0.0027 e[k]u[k] = u[k-1] + 0.20\bigl(e[k] - e[k-1]\bigr) + 0.0027\,e[k]u[k]=u[k−1]+0.20(e[k]−e[k−1])+0.0027e[k]

    Step 2. Check the period: the closed loop has τc=20\tau_c = 20τc​=20 ms, a bandwidth of 1/τc=501/\tau_c = 501/τc​=50 rad/s or 8 Hz. A 1 kHz sampling frequency is 125 times higher, more than enough.

    Step 3. The program in the timer interrupt routine.

    // called every 1 ms from the timer interrupt
void speed_loop(void) {
    float e = speed_ref - speed_meas;        // rad/s
    u += 0.20f * (e - e_prev) + 0.0027f * e; // velocity form
    if (u > 12.0f) u = 12.0f;                // limiting = anti-windup
    if (u < 0.0f)  u = 0.0f;
    e_prev = e;
    set_pwm_duty(u / 12.0f);                 // 0–12 V → duty 0–1
}

    The limiting lines stop u from accumulating beyond the supply rating, so the windup problem found in Step 3 of Example 13.1 is solved with no extra code.

    PID in a PLC

    A PLC PID block uses the same difference equation. The user is responsible for calling the block at a constant period, for example in a cyclic interrupt task and not in the main scan, whose time varies. The block multiplies KiK_iKi​ and divides KdK_dKd​ by the configured TTT. If the real period differs from the configured one, the I and D gains are wrong in proportion.

    Summary of Unit 15

    • A digital loop works in periods: sample with the ADC, compute, then hold the output with a DAC or PWM.
    • Sample at 10–30 times the closed-loop bandwidth and put an anti-aliasing filter in front of the ADC.
    • z−1z^{-1}z−1 is a delay of one period. The system is stable when its poles are inside the unit circle.
    • Sampling adds delay and so limits the usable gain.
    • A velocity-form digital PID takes a few lines of code, and limiting its output prevents windup.
