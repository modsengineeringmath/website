    Graphs of Functions and Transformations: Shifts, Stretches, Reflections, Symmetry, Even and Odd Functions, and Lines

    If you know the shapes of ten parent graphs and understand what the numbers a, b, h and k do in y=a f(b(x−h))+ky = a\,f\big(b(x - h)\big) + ky=af(b(x−h))+k, you can sketch hundreds of other functions without plotting point after point. This page starts by reading a graph for all the information it holds, reviews lines and circles, the graphs you meet most often, and then takes shifts, stretches and reflections one at a time, with a lab where dragging a slider visibly moves each point of the graph.

    Reading a graph completely

    One graph answers many questions at once. The domain is the shadow of the graph on the x-axis and the range is its shadow on the y-axis. The x-intercepts solve f(x)=0f(x) = 0f(x)=0 and the y-intercept is f(0)f(0)f(0). Where the graph climbs as you read left to right the function is increasing, and local maxima and minima are where it turns. This skill matters more than calculation: in calculus you will use derivatives to find these features, and you need to know what you are looking for on the graph.

    G1 Reading a graph

    x-int:f(x)=0,y-int:(0,f(0)),x1<x2⇒f(x1)<f(x2) (increasing)x\text{-int}: f(x) = 0,\quad y\text{-int}: (0, f(0)),\quad x_1 < x_2 \Rightarrow f(x_1) < f(x_2)\ \text{(increasing)}x-int:f(x)=0,y-int:(0,f(0)),x1​<x2​⇒f(x1​)<f(x2​) (increasing)

    Lines, slope and circles

    Slope is rise over run, m=Δy/Δxm = \Delta y/\Delta xm=Δy/Δx, the average rate of change that stays the same along a whole line. A line has several equation forms and each suits different information: with a slope and a point use point–slope form; to read the y-intercept use y=mx+cy = mx + cy=mx+c. The circle is a graph that is not a function, but it is used constantly in analytic geometry and as the unit circle of trigonometry.

    Shifts, stretches and reflections

    Transformations come in two kinds. Those applied outside the function, a and k, act on the y-value directly, so the graph moves vertically just as you would expect. Those applied inside, b and h, act on x before it enters f, so the graph moves horizontally the opposite way: subtracting h moves it right, multiplying by b squeezes it. The reason is that the new graph has to wait until b(x−h)b(x - h)b(x−h) equals the old x to produce the old y.

    Symmetry, even and odd functions

    Knowing that a graph is symmetric halves the work, since you can draw the right half and reflect it. In calculus, symmetry also gives integrals over symmetric intervals for free; the integral of an odd function from −a to a is zero. To test, replace x by −x and compare with the original formula.

    Frequently asked questions

    Why does f(x − 2) shift the graph right and not left?

    y = f(x − 2) reproduces the old value f(0) only when x − 2 = 0, that is at x = 2. Every point of the original graph therefore appears 2 units later, which is 2 units to the right. Changes made to x inside the function always act in the opposite direction to what you might expect, for shifts and for stretches.

    In what order should I apply several transformations?

    For y = a·f(b(x − h)) + k, do the horizontal changes from the inside out (stretch or compress by b, then shift by h) and the vertical changes in the usual order (stretch or reflect by a, then shift by k). The method that never fails is to move each key point (x, y) of the parent graph to (x/b + h, a·y + k).

    How do I recognise even and odd functions?

    An even function satisfies f(−x) = f(x) and is symmetric about the y-axis, like x² and cos x. An odd function satisfies f(−x) = −f(x) and is symmetric about the origin, like x³ and sin x. If replacing x by −x gives neither, the function is neither even nor odd, which is the case for most functions.

    When are two lines perpendicular?

    When the product of their slopes is −1, so one slope is the negative reciprocal of the other: 3/4 is perpendicular to −4/3. The exception is a horizontal line and a vertical line, which are perpendicular although a vertical line has no slope.
